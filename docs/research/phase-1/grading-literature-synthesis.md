## Evidence by design decision

### (a) Graded anchors / few-shot calibration of the grader

**Confidence:** medium

**Recommendation:** Keep anchors as a core design choice and describe the 26% as one study's direction, not a forecast. Use a small fixed set that spans the scale, including a real top-band and a real low-band anchor where they exist, each with a human-confirmed grade and a short rationale. Never let a tool-assigned grade become an anchor until the author or an instructor confirms it, and exclude the draft under review (and near-duplicates) from the anchor set. Seed-randomise or counterbalance anchor order across reviewers and record it. Treat anchor count as a parameter to test against essay length on a gold set, not as 'two per level' fixed in advance. Test the guess-then-reveal pass only as an eval arm.

**Supports**

- Idowu and Almasoud (ASAP 2.0, US school grades 6-10 argumentative essays; 450 test essays; GPT-5.1; 12 calibration essays, 2 per score level): QWK single agent 0.5664 to 0.7165 (+26.5% relative), multi-agent 0.5917 to 0.7453 (+25.96%); exact match 49.11% to 54.00% and 47.56% to 57.11% (Table 1, Sec 4.2). Few-shot was the largest effect measured, far larger than the architecture effect (2.88 QWK points, about 4% relative). Only these two levers were varied, and the authors did not vary the number or selection of examples (checked against the PDF and the project's own claim-check note).
- Kubesch et al. (Austrian A-level German exam scripts, Llama 3.3 70b, at most 101 scripts, one human grader, mostly single runs): every graded-reference-text variant using retrieval beat the rubric-only baseline on final-grade QWK (baseline .29; six retrieval variants .34 to .48; turn-based few-shot .40 to .43 for best-worst and mixed sets). Zero-shot Llama only gave grades 2-4 and never a 1 until it saw calibration examples (Sec 6.1). With the same three anchors, a 'guess the grade, then see the true grade' loop gave higher per-dimension QWK than a static prompt in 7 of 8 cells, though final-grade QWK was lower (.40 vs .46).
- Manakina and Bogdanov (IELTS Task 2, Gemini 2.5 Flash, single run, work in progress): annotated high- and low-scoring example essays gave the largest effect measured, in both multi-step and single-step recipes. Direction only: the MAE and QWK values are approximate figure readings and the paper is internally inconsistent about which prompt variant produced them.
- CoGrader (UIST '25; 5 project reports, 12 raters): regrading against instructor-edited high and low benchmark reports was rated more reliable than the first pass (Q7 6.08 vs Q5 4.91). This is directional only because benchmarks, instructor edits and a second pass are confounded. The useful design detail is that anchors carry confirmed scores plus rationales and the grader is asked how the draft differs from them.

**Challenges**

- No study cleanly varied anchor count or choice. Kubesch: five one-per-grade anchors gave final-grade QWK .25, below the zero-shot baseline .29; doubling a range-of-examples set from 5 to 10 texts lowered final-grade QWK from .46 to .34 even though exact accuracy rose from 28.9% to 30.2%. These runs are confounded by anchor order and by anchors marked 2 and 4, and the authors' 'overfilled context' explanation is untested. So 'more anchors is better' is not supported, and humanities essays are far longer than any text studied, so the context cost of anchors is unmeasured.
- Anchor order and similarity: Kubesch reports, from unreported test runs with no numbers, that order moved bias (best-to-worst skewed low, worst-to-best overrated) and that the grader leaned toward the latest example shown. Retrieving more similar texts did not help (final QWK .48, .47, .38 for 1, 4, 7 texts). Anchors taken from earlier drafts of the same essay are the most similar texts possible and may pull scores toward the earlier grade (inference, untested).
- Leakage and label provenance are unknown: Manakina and Kubesch do not say whether anchor texts were excluded from the scored set; Nguyen et al. do not describe their retrieval corpus, so their large retrieval gains are probably inflated. Nguyen's criterion labels were LLM-generated from the known overall band, a reason to keep anchor grades human-confirmed (weak, speculative).
- Nguyen et al. found fixed two-shot prompting did not help GPT-4o (accuracy 0.71 vs 0.72 zero-shot) and hurt Llama-3-70B (0.56 vs 0.63). Treat as a weak caution only: selection method unstated, metrics undefined, single run.
- Every anchor study is school-level, exam or IELTS material. None involves graduate humanities, long essays or successive drafts of one author. Idowu itself notes single dataset, school-level writers, one model.

### (b) Five-specialist panel with fresh contexts and a deterministic adjudicator versus a single grader

**Confidence:** low

**Recommendation:** Do not claim the panel is more accurate than a single grader; the evidence is mixed and there is none for this exact design. Justify the panel as a diagnostic structure (per-criterion evidence from focused reviewers) and test it: run an A/B on a human-graded gold set (single anchored holistic grader vs panel) and report exact and adjacent agreement, signed bias and spread per band, looking especially at mid-range and top-band drafts. Keep the deterministic adjudicator free of vetoes and minimum rules, give each specialist an explicit ignore list, add a findings filter and ranker for over-flagging, and do not claim fresh contexts remove shared model bias.

**Supports**

- Idowu and Almasoud: three specialists (Content, Structure, Language, each told to ignore the others' dimensions) plus a Chairman with veto and cap heuristics beat the single agent on low-scoring essays (few-shot exact match: score 1, n=30, 73.3% vs 46.7%; score 2, n=120, 65.8% vs 55.0%) and marginally overall (QWK 0.7453 vs 0.7165, about 4% relative). The authors suggest multi-agent suits diagnostic screening of weak essays.
- Manakina and Bogdanov: scoring criteria in separate prompts with programmatic aggregation beat direct overall scoring when anchors were present (approximate MAE 0.85 vs 1.0). Compute is confounded (4 calls vs 1), the advantage appeared only with examples, and paper inconsistencies may shrink the margin (an ablation variant gave MAE 0.965 for the same recipe).
- Nguyen et al. (weak; row mapping inferred; labels built to average to the overall band): four criterion-specific adapters were slightly better than one adapter. EssayCBM: scoring via separate rubric concepts was roughly on par with direct scoring, so decomposition costs little accuracy on one unnamed dataset.
- Karjus et al. and Lu et al. show per-criterion isolation is workable and auditable, but neither compared it with a single call.

**Challenges**

- Idowu: the single agent was marginally ahead on mid-range essays (few-shot exact match score 3, n=168: 54.2% vs 52.4%; score 4, n=103: 61.2% vs 57.3%), which the authors attribute to implicit compensatory scoring in a holistic view. Both architectures struggled on high-scoring essays, and the Chairman's capping logic likely compounded the under-prediction at the top. The multi-agent system also cost 4x (four calls).
- No study tests five fresh-context specialists with a deterministic adjudicator. Idowu's Chairman used veto and cap logic, which the plan's rule deliberately avoids, so Idowu's low-band gain (attributed by the authors to the veto) and its top-band harm may both fail to transfer.
- Shared-model bias: Jadhav et al. found two models from different developers showed the same ordering of style penalties, so same-model fresh contexts decorrelate context, not weights (inference, weak). The FOXGLOVE extraction's claim that same-model reviewers share blind spots did not survive verification (it compared four different model families). The only supported point there is that LLMs over-flag (GPT-5.2 and Claude highlighted over 80% of each essay), so the orchestrator needs a filtering rule.
- Aggregation can flatter: Kubesch's best final-grade QWK (.48) came with low per-dimension accuracy and the authors suspect averaging helped. Overall agreement is not evidence of criterion-level reliability.
- Yu and Rau's very high alpha across six items (above 0.92 to 0.99) is internal consistency, not agreement with humans; the halo reading is only an inference.

### (c) Overall grade as a weighted average of six criterion grades versus a holistic grade

**Confidence:** low

**Recommendation:** Keep the weighted average as the reported grade because it is transparent, editable and traceable, and say in the README that this is a design choice, not an accuracy result. Present the default weights as editable judgement calls. In the eval harness, also compute a holistic cross-check grade and flag drafts where the two differ by a full grade step; report criterion-level agreement as well as overall agreement, and log inter-criterion correlations across drafts to catch double-counting.

**Supports**

- Manakina and Bogdanov computed the overall band in code from separately scored criteria and got lower error than direct overall scoring when anchors were present (approximate figure readings, with the caveats under (b)). Nguyen et al. took an unweighted mean of four criterion scores, but their labels were built to average to the gold overall band, so mean-matching is partly circular.
- EssayCBM computes the grade only from eight inspectable concept scores and lets a human edit one and recompute; accuracy differed from direct grading by -1.31 to +0.96 points and macro-F1 by -3.10 to +2.37 across four encoders (row pairing inferred, no variance), so this is 'competitive', not better. The design pattern (traceable, overridable aggregation) is the transferable part.
- CoGrader participants asked for metric weighting, semantic grouping and a redundancy check across criteria, and Lu et al. aggregated binary rubric items with an instructor weighting scheme. Both are design signals, not tests of aggregation.

**Challenges**

- No paper compares a weighted average with a holistic grade, and none tests editable weights. Karjus et al. deliberately used unweighted sums, so their results say nothing about weighted totals. The default weights in DECISIONS.md D10 (25/25/20/10/10/10) have neither support nor challenge in these papers.
- Idowu: the single-agent holistic grade was marginally better mid-range, which the authors link to compensatory scoring across dimensions.
- Kubesch: overall-grade agreement can run ahead of criterion-level agreement (best final-grade QWK .48 with low per-dimension accuracy; authors suspect averaging or rounding). A weighted average can look reliable while individual criteria are not.
- Criteria may overlap and double-count (Kubesch notes blurred category boundaries in its supervised models; CoGrader participants asked for overlap checks). No evidence either way for argument, structure and originality overlap.

### (d) Quotation-integrity cap applied only for source failures (two grades shown)

**Confidence:** medium

**Recommendation:** Keep the cap narrow and deterministic: trigger it only when a quotation is checked against an original the tool actually obtained and demonstrably does not match, after normalising whitespace, punctuation, hyphenation and conversion artifacts. 'Unverified' (not checked) must never trigger the cap; it stays a label. Show the capped and uncapped grades together with the offending quotations. Separately, run a programmatic verbatim check on every excerpt a reviewer cites from the draft, and drop or relabel failures instead of re-asking the model. Log cap events and measure the checker's false-positive rate on the gold set. Whether the cap lowers the overall grade or only the primary-text and sources criteria is the user's call.

**Supports**

- Idowu: veto and cap rules improved low-band accuracy but the authors say capping likely pulled strong essays down (a specialist's concern about one dimension constrained the holistic score). That favours a cap that is narrow and triggered only by a clear, checkable fault.
- Liu et al. (FOXGLOVE; 69 grade-12 essays): when models were asked to quote spans of the student's essay verbatim, some returned text not in the essay. Cumulative 'span not in essay' failures across retry rounds were Qwen3-Next 80B 65, GPT-5.2 21, Llama 3.3 70B 15, Claude Sonnet 4.5 0; GPT-5.2 and Qwen kept failing a handful of cases at temperature 0 and needed manual fixes. Supports a programmatic check of every quotation the system emits. The counts are cumulative, not per-output rates, and concern models quoting the draft, not authors quoting sources.
- Kubesch (Austrian rubric): a grade of 5 in a main section makes the whole exam negative with limited compensation, a human-system precedent for a floor rule. It was not tested with an LLM applying it.
- CoGrader (R6) and Lu et al.: quoted or highlighted evidence beside each judgment makes errors easier to spot (nine of 12 CoGrader participants reported checking most AI-referenced facts; self-report). No hallucination rate is reported in either.

**Challenges**

- No paper measures how often students misquote sources, or tests a source-fidelity cap's effect on grade accuracy or on authors. Byun et al., Nguyen et al., EssayCBM and Karjus et al. (whose grader trusted short source summaries) say nothing on quotation checking.
- Any cap risks under-grading strong work, the band where models are already weakest (Idowu).
- False positives from the checker could wrongly trigger a cap: Kubesch found OCR artifacts were read as writing mistakes; PDF-to-text conversion could alter quoted text (speculation). 'Could not check' is not the same as 'checked and does not match'.
- Retrying does not reliably repair model misquotation (FOXGLOVE), and the 'two grades shown' format is untested.

### (e) Treating A-range grades as provisional

**Confidence:** medium

**Recommendation:** Keep A-range grades provisional, justified by thin evidence rather than a measured error size. Word the note as 'this grade may be off in either direction and has less evidence behind it than lower bands', show the grade as a band or range, include genuine top-band anchors and say how many exist, and attach the human-check flag. Measure per-band signed bias on the gold set and include a neutral-severity arm against the harsh default before keeping it.

**Supports**

- Idowu and Almasoud: on the second-highest band (score 5 of 6, n=26) exact match was 30.8% for both few-shot setups (7.7% single and 11.5% multi zero-shot); the top band had n=3 and is statistically insufficient (Table 2, Sec 4.1.3). Both systems often gave 3 or 4 to essays humans marked 5 or 6, which the authors call regression to the mean and say the Chairman's capping likely made worse.
- Kubesch et al.: even with retrieved context the models still struggled on perfect or failed exam scripts; zero-shot Llama used only grades 2-4; best final-grade exact match was 32.8% and best sub-dimension 40.6%, and the authors call this too low for real-world grading (single human grader, mostly single runs).
- Karjus et al.: the top band is almost empty in human ground truth (a single assessor gave 27 of 27 to only 7 and 2 of 764 essays, never both), human assessors themselves are noisy (a third review was needed for 5.5% of 7,220 and 8.5% of 7,672 national exam essays; appeals raised the score in 51% of 69 and 60% of 96 cases), and 19 of 20 GPT-4.1-written essays received maximum marks from a GPT-4.1 grader (same-family, no human grading).
- Byun et al.: several rubric sections sat at the maximum for every report from both graders, so identical scores there reflect a ceiling with no discrimination. High-looking agreement at the top can be uninformative.

**Challenges**

- The one direct measurement is a second-highest band on a 1-6 school scale with n=26; nothing reports A-range accuracy on graduate humanities writing, and the size of the effect for stronger models is unknown. Most papers (Karjus, Manakina, Nguyen, Lu, EssayCBM, Byun) report no band-level results at all.
- The sign of top-band error is not constant: Idowu found under-prediction, whereas GPT-4.1 over-predicted on the 12th-grade exam (signed bias +4.68 on 0-27, with prompt wording confounded). 'Provisional' should not imply one direction.
- Exact-grade reliability is weak in every band (Idowu overall exact match 54-57% few-shot; Kubesch 20.8% to 32.8% final-grade; Karjus human noise), so the case for 'provisional' is arguably general, with the top band flagged for extra reasons.
- The project default of harsh grading (DECISIONS.md D13) could compound top-band under-prediction (inference); Karjus found a slightly stronger anti-leniency instruction changed little.

### (f) Never rewriting the author's prose; anchored, goal-oriented feedback

**Confidence:** low

**Recommendation:** Keep 'never write the author's prose' as a design and authorship principle and state it as such, not as an evidence-backed learning benefit. Require every item to cite a quoted passage with its location, limit each report to a few ranked priorities, state plainly what is insufficient in near-miss passages instead of only asking questions, keep reports short, add a lint step that flags report sentences which near-copy author sentences beyond short quoted pointers, and say that the order of priorities is contestable. Pilot reports with real authors, and control for length in any human or LLM-judge evaluation of reports.

**Supports**

- FOXGLOVE (69 grade-12 essays; instructors and four LLMs): feedback was structured as goal-oriented, sentence-anchored and prioritised, motivated by writing research and not tested; both instructors and LLMs were told to suggest improvements without rewriting. No compliance rate for either was reported.
- Lu et al. (FeedbackWriter, intro economics, 354 students, 11 TAs and sections randomised; LLM-scored outcome): AI-assisted TA feedback gave a higher adjusted final-draft score than TA-only feedback (b=0.05, t=5.02, p<0.001; raw means 0.69 vs 0.67). Within the AI condition only the number of adopted AI constructive comments predicted final-draft quality (b=0.005, p=0.027), not praise or generic comments (observational). TAs added cues stating why a near-miss fell short because hints were too generic.
- CoGrader (interviews and Likert, 12 raters): anecdotes that generic, AI-styled comments are noticed and disliked and that instructors want origin labelled; participants asked for comments linked to report text so they can be checked.
- Yu and Rau (about 500 lay raters per questionnaire; conflict-advice replies, not essay feedback): trust and perceived competence rose from critical to neutral or affirmative replies and then fell for sycophantic and hyper-sycophantic replies (trust F(4,9009)=61.345; competence F=52.003). Supports critical-but-not-effusive tone as a perception result only.

**Challenges**

- No study tests that withholding rewrites improves revisions or learning, and none tests unrelated-topic illustrations. In Lu et al. the post-test showed no condition effect (b=-0.007, p=0.31), the revision effect came from human-reviewed AI on a checklist-style task scored by an LLM on the same rubric pipeline, and the authors say the approach likely fits less well for dialogic, reflexive or rhetorical writing (Sec 7.5), which is close to philosophy and critical theory.
- Hint-style feedback can be too vague for near-miss passages (Lu et al., anecdotal); the hint-quality metrics were LLM-tagged on TA-edited final feedback and mirror the AI prompt's instructions, so the advantage is partly by construction.
- LLM feedback is long and over-flags: sentence-level comments averaged 83.64 words vs 34.70 for instructors; GPT-5.2 and Claude highlighted 88.3% and 82.1% of each essay vs 42.8% for instructors. Raters scored LLM feedback higher on six of seven dimensions, but the specificity gap fell from 1.85 to 0.45 and the actionability gap from 1.40 to 0.42 at equal length (pooled regression, 85 items, 5 essays, 2 raters, weighted kappa 0.36, no tests, and length is confounded with source). Thorough-looking is not shown to be better, and TAs said students skip long comments.
- Prioritisation is unstable: among overlapping comment pairs where both gave a rank, exact agreement on urgency tier was 38.5% (mean rank difference 0.79). A 'what it would take to reach the next grade' list is one defensible ordering among several.
- Readability formulas did not track clarity ratings (LLM feedback Flesch-Kincaid about 14.4 vs 8.5 but rated clearer), and default LLM output was less conversational (0.23 vs 0.85 question marks per 100 words), so hedged, plain, non-moralising tone must be specified and checked, not assumed.

### (g) Guarding against sycophancy and against style, length and fluency bias

**Confidence:** medium

**Recommendation:** Do not rely on an instruction in the reviewer prompt ('ignore style', 'do not be lenient') as the defence. Add structural measures: separate criteria so that only mechanics and voice-echo own surface quality, hide earlier grades and the author's self-assessment from reviewers, delimit the draft as untrusted data and flag text addressed to the grader, and run a shipped perturbation self-test (grammar, register, ESL phrasing, padding, polish) with a human-checked content-equivalence step, reporting raw point change per criterion. Measure signed bias against the gold set per model version, and guard against both leniency and harshness.

**Supports**

- Jadhav et al. (60 synthetic short essays and other tasks; LLaMA 3.3 70B and Qwen 2.5 72B; n=20 per cell; temperature about 0): with content intended to be unchanged, essay rewrites scored lower despite explicit instructions not to penalise grammar, informality or non-standard English. Informal -1.90 (LLaMA) and -1.20 (Qwen); non-native phrasing -1.35 and -0.90; grammar errors -0.60 and -0.30 on a 10-point scale; all six essay cells p<=.010. Content equivalence was not validated and part of the penalty may be legitimate quality loss.
- Karjus et al. (Estonian school exam essays; GPT-4.1): appending one sentence demanding the maximum score raised totals by 6.43 points on average (n=100, min 2, max 16, 0-27 scale), with grading instructions in the system prompt and the essay in the user prompt; no defended condition was tested. A 'slightly stronger' anti-leniency instruction changed little (12th-grade bias +4.68 to +4.27; MAE 4.97 to 4.69; one model, one cohort, one run).
- Kubesch: order of anchors shifted leniency or strictness (qualitative, numbers not reported). Yu and Rau: perceived sycophancy of generated replies differed strongly across ten models (F(9,944)=134.052), rated only by LLM judges and for conflict advice, not grading.
- Lu et al.: TAs said the AI was stricter than they were and passed imprecise near-misses less often than hoped (interview-based; no direction-of-override data).

**Challenges**

- No paper tests whether length or fluency inflates an LLM grader (Jadhav only degraded a polished base; the FOXGLOVE length result concerns raters of feedback comments), and none tests whether a grader drifts toward the author's own claims. The plan's specific safeguards were not tested anywhere.
- The direction of bias is not constant: GPT-4.1 signed bias +1.71 (9th grade) and +4.68 (12th grade) and Gemini 2 Flash +1.73 (12th grade) against a human mean, with prompt wording differing between grades (9th-grade prompts contain leniency nudges); GPT-4o at temperature 0 graded lower than the TA in 38.8% of quiz scores and higher in 6.2% (Byun et al.); Idowu found under-prediction at the top. A leniency-only guard is incomplete.
- Jadhav and Karjus used open or older models and small synthetic or exam samples with no no-instruction control, so they show instructions were insufficient there, not by how much. Transfer to the model the plugin will actually run is untested.
- Human labels used as ground truth may themselves reward length and lexical surface (Karjus Table 3, alignment inferred), so agreement with humans cannot validate resistance to style bias.

### (h) Showing confidence and a human-check flag

**Confidence:** medium

**Recommendation:** Show confidence as a categorical note (low, medium, high) with named reasons drawn from observable signals: reviewer disagreement, spread across repeated runs (repeat only drafts near a band boundary or in the top band), unverified-quotation share, criterion coverage, and distance from the anchors. Add a human-check flag for top-band results, any cap, reviewer disagreement of one band or more, and high run-to-run spread. Do not show numeric probabilities. State that the notes are uncalibrated until the gold-set run shows that low-confidence drafts have larger errors.

**Supports**

- Kubesch: best final-grade exact match 32.8% and best sub-dimension 40.6%, with the authors recommending human oversight. Karjus: only 36-68% of model totals fell inside the human range (Table 1), against the paper's own summary claim that models stay within it.
- Byun et al.: pooled Pearson r 0.982 coexisted with 55% exact agreement and a systematic offset (paired t -8.960), so a single headline number hides error.
- CoGrader (12 raters, 5 reports): instructors adjusted or overrode 64.09% of AI scores and comments on subjective metrics that the workflow itself flagged for review (unit unstated, direction unreported); one participant saw score variation across attempts. Lu et al.: TAs overrode about 11% of AI judgments by the paper's text (its own tables imply a lower count); the authors propose per-rubric confidence, untested.
- Yu and Rau recommend human validation or several evaluator models over a single judge (untested), and repeated ratings at temperature 1.0 were used to stabilise outputs (no variance reported).

**Challenges**

- No study validates a confidence mechanism for essay grades. Run-to-run variance is unmeasured in Karjus, Byun et al. and Kubesch (mostly single runs), although Kubesch's own tables show unexplained differences of about 0.1 QWK between near-identical settings.
- EssayCBM's demo screenshot shows a final grade 3/6 with 'Confidence: 19.0%' and flat concept probabilities, a raw class probability presented alongside a clean grade with no calibration analysis. A displayed number can look rigorous and be meaningless.
- CoGrader and Lu et al. rely on a human with final authority. betterpaper has no instructor in the loop, and override rates there are not accuracy figures.
- The 'in human range' metric (Karjus) is lenient and correlation hides bias, so these papers' agreement figures cannot be used to set thresholds.

### (i) Cost of the full five-reviewer panel versus quick mode

**Confidence:** low

**Recommendation:** Log tokens per reviewer and per draft from the first run and print an estimated cost before a full panel run. Define quick mode as a single anchored grader plus the deterministic quotation check and mechanics tools, reporting through the same template and confidence note with a plain banner that no specialist cross-check ran. Order each reviewer prompt with the shared rubric and anchors first and the reviewer-specific instructions last, so a cache can help if the harness supports it. Decide the default (DECISIONS.md D2) after the gold-set A/B, not before.

**Supports**

- Idowu and Almasoud: the multi-agent system needed four API calls against one, and the authors say a 4% relative QWK gain may not justify 4x cost in all deployments (Sec 4.3).
- Karjus et al.: nine isolated calls per essay turned 400,433 essay tokens into 9,098,937 total tokens for 781 short essays (about 510 tokens each), roughly USD 20 on GPT-4.1, with batch mode halving it. The verifier showed the 22.7x multiplier comes from short essays plus repeated prompt overhead; for long drafts the multiplier approaches the number of reviewers (about 5x for five, my extraction's own arithmetic).
- Manakina and Bogdanov: multi-step with examples cost about USD 0.0011 per essay against 0.0003 for single-step with examples (about 3.7x, approximate figure readings, Gemini 2.5 Flash, short IELTS essays; the paper's cost figures are mutually inconsistent). Kubesch: latency rose from about 240 s to up to 750 s per task pair with large-context methods on a self-hosted 70b model (direction only). Byun et al.: caching a fixed prefix cut a quiz-grading estimate by about a third (back-of-envelope, short prompts).

**Challenges**

- No study measures accuracy per unit cost for a panel versus a single grader on long essays, and no study compares a quick mode with a full panel for humanities drafts. None reports token cost for five reviewers with anchors.
- Idowu suggests the benefit depends on draft quality (panel better on weak essays, single grader marginally better mid-range), so a blanket default may be wrong in either direction. Anchors multiply input cost per reviewer unless a shared prefix is cached, and long humanities drafts plus anchors may be much larger than anything studied.
- The bandit method in Manakina and Bogdanov needs a human grade for every essay to compute its reward, so it cannot choose a recipe on a user's new drafts; its headline savings concern development-time recipe selection and are overstated by unequal essay counts (500 vs 787).

## Claims to avoid

- That giving anchors raises grader accuracy by about 26% in general. It is a relative QWK gain from one study (US school argumentative essays, one model, 12 calibration essays, one run). Say 'in one published study'.
- That two anchors per score level is optimal, or that more anchors do not help or always help. Idowu did not vary the number or choice of examples; Kubesch's five-anchor and ten-text results are confounded; Nguyen's shots table contains an impossible cell.
- That similar anchors (for example earlier drafts of the same essay) work better than a fixed spanning set, or the reverse. Kubesch's similarity results are mixed and Nguyen's retrieval gains are likely inflated by undescribed retrieval and possible leakage.
- That a panel of five reviewers is more accurate than a single grader. Idowu shows a mixed picture (better on weak essays, marginally ahead of the single agent overall, marginally behind it mid-range) for a different design with an LLM chairman applying veto and cap rules.
- That 'single-agent beats multi-agent'. In Idowu the multi-agent system was better on weak essays and slightly better overall; the single agent led by 2 to 4 points on scores 3 and 4 only.
- That fresh-context reviewers from one model remove shared model bias or blind spots. Jadhav's two-model comparison is only suggestive, and the FOXGLOVE claim to that effect failed verification.
- That a weighted average is more accurate, fairer or more consistent than a holistic grade, or that the default weights are evidence-based. No paper tests either.
- That deterministic aggregation of criterion grades is 'validated' or 'stable'. EssayCBM claims a stable, auditable mapping but reports no stability, variance or user test, and the form of its aggregation is not described.
- That the quotation-integrity cap is supported by evidence. No paper measures fabricated or misattributed quotations by authors, nor any cap on source failures. Idowu shows caps can pull strong essays down.
- That LLMs often fabricate quotations. FOXGLOVE shows occasional non-verbatim spans with a few persistent cases, in cumulative retry counts, for models quoting the draft, not authors quoting sources.
- That A-range grades are X% accurate or that LLMs are weak at the top band in general. The only direct figure is the second-highest band of a 1-6 school scale (n=26, 30.8%); the highest band had n=3.
- That LLM graders are lenient, or that they are strict. The sign of bias depends on the model, cohort, prompt and scale: Karjus +1.71 to +4.68 (confounded by prompt wording), Byun et al. mostly under-grading, Idowu under-predicting the top.
- That 'do not be lenient' or 'ignore style' instructions work, or that they do not work. Jadhav has no no-instruction control and Karjus tested a slight instruction tweak on one model. The evidence shows insufficiency in those setups, not the size of any reduction.
- That length or fluency bias in LLM graders is established. Jadhav only degraded polished answers, and FOXGLOVE's length finding concerns raters of feedback, not graders. Also do not describe FOXGLOVE as showing 'length bias in raters'; it shows an association confounded with source.
- That LLM-written or polished text proves fluency leniency. Karjus's 19 of 20 maximum marks used the same model family as writer and grader and had no human grading.
- That the sycophancy scale (PASS) or the five validation levels are validated for essay feedback or for grading. They were built for conflict-advice replies; discriminant validity was not reported and the LLM-versus-human comparison is not in the main text.
- That hint-style or Socratic feedback improves revision or learning. In FeedbackWriter the post-test was null, quality metrics mirror the AI prompt and were measured on TA-edited feedback, and the revision effect rests on human-reviewed AI on checklist tasks scored by an LLM.
- That the d=0.50 effect from FeedbackWriter, its 88.7% TA approval rate, or its 84.6% scorer accuracy are expected performance for betterpaper. Domain, human review and outcome scoring all differ.
- That plain-language, hedged, non-moralising reports raise author uptake or improve the next draft. No paper tests this; Yu and Rau's trust result is from lay raters reading conflict advice.
- That thorough or long feedback is better feedback, or that LLM feedback is better than expert feedback. FOXGLOVE's specificity and actionability gaps shrink at equal length and rest on 5 essays and 2 raters.
- That a confidence note, an A-range flag or a displayed confidence is calibrated. No paper validates one; EssayCBM's demo shows a raw probability of 19.0% beside a clean grade.
- That 64.09% (CoGrader) or 11.3% (FeedbackWriter) override rates measure the AI's error rate or the share of 'interpretive' criteria needing review. CoGrader's subjective metrics were flagged for review by design, and the verifier dropped any claim about which criteria need the most review.
- That a high correlation shows good agreement. Byun et al.'s r=0.982 coexists with 55% exact agreement and a significant offset; CoGrader's correlations are for humans scoring after using the tool, not the LLM alone.
- That 'identical scores' at the top mean the grader is reliable there. In Byun et al. these are ceiling effects with no discrimination.
- That deterministic tools plus an LLM make a better mechanics reviewer. Karjus's supervised-model advantage on mechanics was downgraded after verification (continuous vs integer scores, trained on the same labels with no holdout, features not purely deterministic).
- That the panel costs about 22.7x a single call (Karjus) or about 5x (an extrapolation from my extraction's arithmetic), or that caching saves a third for long essays. These come from short essays, short quizzes or back-of-envelope estimates.
- That the bandit method can pick the best grading recipe at run time on a user's drafts. Its reward needs a human grade for every essay; its 78.4% call saving also compares 500 essays with 787.
- That LLM graders 'always stay within the human range' (Karjus summary). Table 1 shows 36-68% of totals inside the range.
- Exact MAE, QWK or accuracy values from Nguyen et al. and Manakina and Bogdanov (figure readings, undefined metrics, impossible or inconsistent table cells); and any headline from Nguyen's Table 3, which is garbled in extraction and whose row mapping is inferred.
- That Idowu's finding transfers to Claude models or to graduate humanities writing. It used GPT-5.1 and school essays and the authors flag single dataset, school-level writers and one model.

## Design changes suggested by the evidence

1. Anchors (skills/init/templates/RUBRIC.md and STATE.md, skills/grade/SKILL.md, grade-bands.md): add an anchor record with grade, who confirmed it (author or instructor, never the tool alone), a two-to-three-line rationale, and its band. Require at least one real top-band and one low-band anchor where such essays exist, and report how many anchors sit near the top band. Exclude the draft being graded and its near-duplicates. Make anchor count a setting, not a fixed 'two per level'.
2. Anchor order (grade SKILL.md): pass a seeded, recorded permutation of the anchors to each reviewer, and add an eval that checks whether grades drift toward the last-shown anchor.
3. Optional calibration pass (evals/): add a 'guess the anchor's grade, then see the true grade' arm and compare it with static anchors on the gold set. Do not ship it as default.
4. Comparative feedback (agents/*.md, report-template.md): ask each reviewer to say, with a quoted passage, how the draft differs from the nearest anchors above and below. This is a CoGrader design idea and untested, but it fits the plan to say what it would take to reach the next grade.
5. Adjudication (grade/references/rules.md): write the weighted average, rounding rule and the only cap down in full. The cap fires only when the source-verifier marks a quotation as checked against an obtained original and not matching; 'unverified' never caps. Trigger extra scrutiny only when reviewers differ by a band or more. No veto and no minimum rules. Record the second grade ('as submitted' vs 'on the writing and argument') with the offending quotations.
6. Cited-span check (new script, for example scripts/check_cited_spans.py, plus the reviewers' output schema): every excerpt a reviewer returns must appear verbatim in the draft after normalisation; failures are dropped or labelled as paraphrase, not re-asked. Extend scripts/extract_quotes.py to normalise whitespace, hyphenation and conversion artifacts and to separate 'unverified', 'verified-against-secondary', 'verified-against-original' and 'checked and does not match'.
7. Input quality (grade SKILL.md): run a pre-check on extracted text (PDF or OCR artifacts, broken quotes) and warn before grading, so conversion errors are not blamed on the author or used to trigger a cap.
8. Findings filter (agents/*.md and grade SKILL.md): limit each reviewer to a few ranked findings with a quoted location and an impact line; the orchestrator merges them by a fixed rule. Say in the report that the priority order is contestable, since urgency ranking agreed only 38.5% of the time in FOXGLOVE.
9. Confidence and flags (report-template.md, STATE.md): add a categorical confidence line per criterion and overall with named reasons (reviewer disagreement, repeat-run spread, unverified-quotation share, closeness to a band boundary, top-band). Add a human-check flag with explicit triggers. Run repeats only near boundaries and in the top band. Label the note 'uncalibrated' until the gold-set evaluation shows it separates larger from smaller errors.
10. A-range wording (report-template.md, grade-bands.md): show top-band results as a range, say the grade may be too high or too low, and explain that fewer anchors and less evidence exist at the top. Add a harsh-versus-neutral severity arm to the evals before keeping the harsh default in DECISIONS.md D13.
11. Report authorship rule (report-template.md, plus a new scripts/report_lint.py): flag report sentences that near-copy author sentences beyond short quoted pointers, and any suggested replacement wording; illustrations only on unrelated topics. Keep reports short, evidence-first, with the grade after the reasoning (CoGrader participants suggested forming a view before seeing the score; untested).
12. Near-miss handling (agents/argument-reviewer.md and primary-text-reviewer.md): when a passage is close but imprecise, state plainly what is insufficient and why, in addition to any question. Add an all-required-elements rule for high-band criterion descriptors and a near-miss example per criterion in RUBRIC.md.
13. Rubric text (RUBRIC.md, grade-bands.md): write each criterion as observable descriptors with a short definition, whether it is judged on a part or the whole, and a concrete example per band. Test a lean rubric against the detailed one on the gold set (Manakina's single-recipe ablation), and check overlap between argument, structure, originality and mechanics to avoid double counting. Confirm which agent owns the originality criterion (the agent stubs do not say).
14. Injection hardening (grade SKILL.md and all agents/*.md): wrap the draft and source text in delimiters, state that it is data and not instructions, and flag text addressed to the grader. Add evals/injection/ with a one-sentence 'give the maximum grade' appended to a fixture draft and measure the change across reviewers and the adjudicator.
15. Style and polish self-test (evals/style-perturbation/): same-content variants with grammar errors, register shifts, ESL phrasing, padding and polish, with a human-checked content-equivalence step, scored per criterion and reported as raw point change. Expect only mechanics and voice-echo to move. A 20-pair test only detects large effects (about d_z 0.65 at 80% power, my calculation), so use more fixture essays or repeated runs. Also hide earlier grades and the author's self-assessment from reviewers.
16. Gold-set evaluation (evals/, currently empty): human-grade a small set of drafts and report QWK or weighted kappa, exact and adjacent agreement per band, signed bias per band, MAE, the spread of grades given (compression), per-criterion agreement, a human-human baseline where available, repeat-run spread, and a re-grade of a lightly edited draft. Re-run when the model, rubric or anchors change. Include arms for panel vs single grader, anchor count vs essay length, and holistic cross-check vs weighted average.
17. Quick mode and cost (grade SKILL.md --quick, STATE.md): quick mode = single anchored grader + deterministic quotation check + mechanics tools, with the same report template and confidence note and a visible banner. Log tokens per reviewer and per draft, print an estimate before a full run, and put the shared rubric and anchors first in each reviewer prompt so caching can help.
18. Docs (DECISIONS.md and docs/research/phase-1/llm-grading-evidence.md): record the evidence-based changes as new decisions. Soften the existing note's line that Idowu 'supports the reviewer split' to match Table 2 (weak-band gain, marginal overall gain, marginal mid-range loss, 4x cost), and keep the 26% and 31% wording exactly as that note already has it.

## Known limits for the README

- Grader calibration is based on one published study on US school essays (a relative QWK gain of about 26% from two worked examples per score level). There is no published test on graduate humanities writing, so treat this as a direction, not a forecast.
- Until a gold-set evaluation is run and published, betterpaper's grades have not been validated against human graders on philosophy or critical-theory drafts. Nothing in this evidence says how close they will be.
- Grades near the top are the least reliable: in the one study that measured it, exact agreement on the second-highest band was about 31% (26 essays), and the models tended to under-score strong essays. Treat A-range grades as provisional, and note the error may run in either direction.
- Even in other bands, exact-grade agreement in published studies is modest (roughly one-third to just over half), and human markers disagree with each other. A grade is an estimate, not a verdict.
- LLM graders in published studies marked down grammar errors, informal register and non-native phrasing even when told not to, and were swayed by a single instruction hidden in the text. betterpaper cannot promise immunity. Non-native writers and unconventional registers are the highest-risk cases.
- Whether a grader is too lenient or too strict varies by model, prompt and task. betterpaper does not assume a direction and the default severity setting has not yet been tested.
- The five-reviewer panel has not been shown to grade more accurately than a single grader. It is there to give focused, evidence-based diagnosis per criterion, and it costs more than quick mode (roughly one call per reviewer, plus the shared context each time).
- Quotations are checked only against originals the tool can reach. 'Unverified' means not checked, not wrong. Text extracted from PDFs or scans can contain artifacts that look like errors.
- Repeated runs on the same draft may give different grades. The confidence note is a plain-language flag built from observable signals, not a calibrated probability, until it is validated.
- No study yet shows that this style of feedback (diagnosis, explanation, pointers, no rewriting) improves the next draft. In the closest related trial the effect on revisions was measured by an LLM on a checklist task, and the effect on later test scores was not detected.
- The order of 'what to fix first' is a judgement call. In one study, human experts agreed on the urgency tier of the same comment only about 38% of the time.
- There is no human reviewer in the loop. Published tools that pair an LLM with an instructor have had the instructor override a substantial share of the model's judgments.

## Open questions

- Can you build a small gold set of human-graded drafts for the evaluation (who confirms each grade, how many drafts, and how many near the top band)? Without one, bias, confidence and the panel-versus-single question cannot be answered for your genre.
- Should D2 (full panel by default, --quick single pass) stay as it is until the gold-set A/B is run? Idowu's evidence suggests the panel helps most on weak drafts and may be marginally worse mid-range, at about four times the calls.
- The harsh default (D13) is now a hypothesis, since models under-predicted the top band in the one top-band study and a slight instruction tweak changed little elsewhere. Are you willing to test a neutral-severity arm and let the result decide?
- Should the quotation-integrity cap fire only when a quotation was checked against an obtained original and does not match, never for 'unverified'? And should it lower the overall grade, or only the primary-text and sources criteria?
- Are anchor grades human-confirmed (by you or an instructor), or could some be tool-assigned grades from earlier drafts? Tool-assigned anchors risk compounding the tool's own errors.
- Should reviewers be blind to earlier drafts' grades and to your own self-assessment, with only the orchestrator comparing drafts? This trades some draft-to-draft continuity for less anchoring (the evidence is weak either way).
- Is it acceptable to show top-band results as a range with the wording 'may be too high or too low', and to attach a human-check flag automatically for top-band results, caps and reviewer disagreement?
- Which agent owns the originality criterion in D10 (weight 10)? The agent stubs list primary-text, argument (with structure), source-verifier, mechanics and voice-echo reviewers, but none is described as reviewing originality.
- Should the perturbation and injection self-tests ship with the plugin for users to run, or stay as development-time evals only? Also, what per-draft token or cost budget is acceptable, and which model will the plugin run on? Almost all the evidence is on non-Claude or older models.
