## 1. Rubrics and guides surveyed

Source IDs (S1 to S8 are graded rubrics, H1 to H4 are writing guides) are reused as tags in section 2.

**S1. AAC&U VALUE Critical Thinking** (4 levels: Capstone 4, Milestones 3 and 2, Benchmark 1).
- URL: https://www.aacu.org/initiatives/value-initiative/value-rubrics/value-rubrics-critical-thinking [M]. Readable copy: https://assessment.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk5396/files/files/page/VALUE_Rubric-Critical_Thinking.pdf
- Criteria [V]: Explanation of Issues; Evidence; Influence of Context and Assumptions; Student's Position (thesis); Conclusions and Related Outcomes.
- Top vs bottom [V]: Capstone states the issue comprehensively, interprets and evaluates sources enough to synthesise, analyses its own and others' assumptions, takes an imaginative position that acknowledges its limits and absorbs other viewpoints, and ties conclusions to a range of information including opposing views, ranked by priority. Benchmark states the issue without clarification and takes a simplistic position.
- Caveat [V]: the VALUE rubrics are for programme-level assessment "not for grading", and are developmental rather than A to F. Useful for subject-neutral language, not for calibrating grades.

**S2. AAC&U VALUE Written Communication** (same 4 levels).
- URL: https://www.aacu.org/initiatives/value-initiative/value-rubrics/value-rubrics-written-communication [M]. Copy: https://assessment.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk5396/files/files/page/VALUE_Rubric-Written_Communication.pdf
- Criteria [V]: Context of and Purpose for Writing; Content Development; Genre and Disciplinary Conventions; Sources and Evidence; Control of Syntax and Mechanics.
- Shape of levels [M]: Capstone is "thorough" understanding of audience and purpose, content that shapes the whole work, skilful use of credible sources, and graceful, nearly error-free prose. Benchmark is minimal attention to audience, simple ideas in parts, an attempt to use sources, and language that sometimes impedes meaning.
- Key point for experimental writing [V]: genre conventions are defined as the "formal and informal rules" for a given kind of text (lab report, academic paper, poetry, personal essay). Assessment is genre-relative by design.

**S3. Caston, "Grading Standards" (Univ. of Arizona philosophy)**. An apparently identical text is hosted by Amherst (authorship of the Amherst copy not verified).
- URLs: https://philosophy.arizona.edu/sites/philosophy.arizona.edu/files/Caston%2C%20Grading%20Standards.pdf and https://www.amherst.edu/system/files/media/0364/Grading%20Standards_0.pdf
- B is the baseline for competent work [V]: addresses all parts of the topic, gives consistent arguments for a clearly stated position, considers significant objections and replies, uses readings relevantly without merely replicating them, no significant misunderstandings, well written with few errors.
- B+ [V]: a B plus the beginnings of an original idea (apt analogy, clever counterexample, sharp distinction that does philosophical work, subtle point from close reading) or ordinary ideas worked out to unusual depth.
- A/A- [V]: works the original or powerful idea out fully and deeply, showing a firm grasp of the underlying concepts, principles and argumentative strategy, or gives an unusually comprehensive survey.
- Lower bands were not in the summary; check the PDF.

**S4. Lake Forest College, "General Grading Criteria for the Essay"** (non-philosophy, widely copied, all five grades defined).
- URL: https://lakeforest.edu/live/files/general-grading-criteria-for-the-essay.pdf
- A [V]: original, logical, coherently organised ideas; clear and persuasive argument; clearly articulated, with only the words the ideas require; paragraphs with distinct but related roles, each holding one thoroughly asserted idea; accurate, thoughtful use of sources; no errors an exacting proofread would catch.
- B [V]: very good, solid persuasive thinking, clearly and effectively executed. What prevents an A is a lack of originality, thorough thinking or careful proofreading.
- C [V]: some conspicuous flaw: underdeveloped argument, disorganisation, consistently inarticulate diction, or in dire need of proofreading.
- D [V]: more than one of the C problems, or otherwise shows the author did not spend nearly enough time thinking or writing. F [V]: misses on all criteria (articulateness, persuasiveness, organisation, absence of writing mistakes).

**S5. Princeton Classics Department, "Standards for Essays, Papers, and Independent Work"** (humanities, full +/- ladder).
- URL: https://classics.princeton.edu/document/5566
- A [V]: argument that is original in the sense of going beyond interpretations available to the student, persuasive, with broad knowledge of the material; "the instructor ought to have learned something".
- A- [V]: excels at two of three A criteria (originality, persuasiveness, range of knowledge) or A work hampered by unclear writing.
- B [V]: a clear argument and good knowledge of the material and received interpretations. B+ [V]: a B with some A-quality observations. B- [V]: just short on knowledge or understanding of the evidence, or B-quality work that lacks a clear argument, is padded with irrelevancies, or is poorly written.
- C [V]: acceptable knowledge but does not reach B in grasping significance or in formulating an argument.
- Useful as the model for how +/- modifiers work (see section 2).

**S6. MIT OCW 24.230 Meta-Ethics, grading criteria for essays** (undergraduate philosophy).
- URL: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/24-230-meta-ethics-fall-2015/pages/assignments/grading-criteria
- A [V]: mastery of the issue and the literature, originality, clarity and precision; a substantive thesis with a persuasive case; anticipates strong and sensible objections and gives compelling replies. B [V]: understanding of the issue but lacking in some degree one or more A features. C [V]: confusions about the issue and material, worse than B on the same dimensions.

**S7. Sacramento State Philosophy, "Grading Standards for Philosophy Papers"**.
- URL: https://www.csus.edu/college/arts-letters/philosophy/grading-standards.html
- A [V]: thesis unambiguously stated; coherently organised so the reader can easily follow the flow of thought; every point relevant to and supporting the thesis; all points essential to the thesis are made; few or no mechanical errors; effort beyond the minimum.
- B [V]: worthwhile effort but not as clear or complete as it could be, some errors. C [V]: generally correct and effective, with inconsistencies in grammar, logic or tone that are repairable without major rewriting.

**S8. Jim Pryor, "Guidelines on Writing a Philosophy Paper"** (the standard student guide).
- URL: https://www.jimpryor.net/teaching/guidelines/writing.html (mirror: https://www.uwo.ca/philosophy/undergraduate/Jim-Pryors-Guidelines-and-Writing-a-Philosophy-Paper-2.pdf). Not a rubric; it states what a good paper does.
- [V]: a philosophy paper is the reasoned defence of a claim; it puts a thesis or argument on the table early; raising objections is often the best way to defend a claim.
- [M]: give the reader a roadmap, define terms, expound before criticising, be charitable, quote sparingly and explain every quotation, say things plainly and avoid grand openers.

**H1 to H4, writing guides (not rubrics, but they fix what "good structure" means in philosophy).**
- H1 Harvard philosophy dept, "Brief Guide to Writing the Philosophy Paper": https://philosophy.fas.harvard.edu/files/phildept/files/brief_guide_to_writing_philosophy_paper.pdf. [V] state a precise thesis clearly and concisely in the introduction; the method is explain the thesis, argue for it, raise an objection, reply.
- H2 Pomona, "Suggestions for Writing Philosophy Papers" parts 1 and 2: https://www.pomona.edu/node/4613 and https://www.pomona.edu/node/4640. [V] roadmap, then Part 1 explicate the philosopher's argument (terms, premises, how the conclusion follows), Part 2 object (reject a premise, or show the conclusion absurd); explanations should be full and economical.
- H3 Vanderbilt Writing Studio: https://www.vanderbilt.edu/writing/resources/handouts/writing-philosophy-papers/. [V] define key terms and use them consistently; even when asked to evaluate, spend serious time on exposition first; objections may be woven through rather than saved for one section; argue against the most cogent version of the objection.
- H4 Yablo (MIT), "Guidelines on Writing a Philosophy Paper": https://web.mit.edu/~yablo/www/writing.html. [V] state the central problem early and why it is a problem; write for a reader who has not read the material, which forces you to explain technical terms.
- The UNC Writing Center philosophy handout (https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/philosophy/) came up but no content was retrievable, so it is not used. Harvard's guidance here is the philosophy department guide (H1), not the Writing Center.

**What separates A, B, C, D, F across these sources**
- A: original or illuminating (the reader learns something), persuasive, accurate, and well organised, with prose that needs no further work (S3, S4, S5, S6, S7).
- B: competent and complete, no significant misunderstanding, considers objections, but lacks originality, depth or polish (S3, S4, S6). Treat B as the baseline for competent work, so A stays rare.
- C: a conspicuous flaw in one area (underdeveloped argument, disorganisation, confusions about the material, proofreading) (S4, S6, S5).
- D: several C-level flaws at once, or visible lack of effort (S4). F: fails on essentially all criteria, or no evidence of the skills at all (S4).
- Padding appears explicitly as a downgrade reason (S5 "padded with irrelevancies", S4 "only the words ideas require"). Originality is judged relative to what was available to the student, not to the field (S5).

## 2. Draft descriptors (guidance only)

Tags show which source each phrase draws on. Criteria split: Command of primary texts = the text(s) under study. Use of sources = secondary literature and other evidence. Argument = whether the reasoning is sound and defended. Structure and organisation = how the material is arranged (owned by argument-reviewer). Keep Argument and Structure from penalising the same flaw twice.

### 2.1 Command of primary texts
- A: Reads the text accurately and closely; claims about what the author says rest on well-chosen, correctly contextualised quotation or reference; notices tensions or competing readings and justifies the reading chosen. [S3 close reading and no misunderstandings, S5 range of knowledge, S6 mastery, S2 content development]
- B: Accurate and relevant use of the text with no significant misreading; interpretation is sound but follows received readings, or textual evidence is thinner than it could be. [S3 B, S5 B]
- C: Grasps the main claims but with some confusion, leaning on summary or loose paraphrase, or asserting what the text says without support. [S6 C, S5 C]
- D: Significant misreadings, or the text is mentioned rather than used (a few loosely connected quotations). [S4 D adapted, own]
- F: Little or no sign the text was read; attributes claims it does not make or invents quotations or content. [own]

### 2.2 Argument
- A: Defends a clear, substantive thesis with valid or well-supported reasoning; defines key terms; anticipates the strongest objections, states them charitably and answers them; distinguishes what it establishes from what it assumes. [S3, S6, S1 position and assumptions, S8, H3]
- B: Clear thesis and coherent reasoning with significant objections considered; some premises left undefended, replies partial, or the conclusion slightly overreaches. [S3 B, S6 B]
- C: Thesis present but underdeveloped; gaps or non-sequiturs; objections missing or handled as straw men; assertion stands in for support. [S4 C, S1 benchmark]
- D: Thesis vague or shifting; mostly assertion, description or opinion; more than one C-level problem. [S4 D]
- F: No discernible argument, or an argument that does not address the question. [S4 F, own]

### 2.3 Structure and organisation (argument-reviewer)
- A: The thesis or governing question appears early and precisely, a roadmap says what comes in what order, and the essay follows it. Each paragraph has one job serving the thesis; premises are ordered so each rests only on what is already established; transitions state the logical relation (because, so, however); proportion is sound, with the strongest objection given enough room to be felt and answered; the conclusion says what was shown and adds no new claim. [S4 paragraph roles, S7 A, S8, H1, H2, H4, S2 genre conventions; proportion and conclusion rule: own]
- B: Clear thesis and generally logical order; paragraphs mostly unified; occasional weak transition, a premise used before it is motivated, or uneven proportion (objection handled briefly); the reader is never lost. [S7 B, S4 B]
- C: Thesis present but buried, vague or revealed only at the end; paragraphs hold several ideas or digress; the reader must reconstruct the order of premises; signposting absent or empty ("this essay will discuss"); long exposition and a token objection. [S4 C disorganised, S7 C, S5 B- padding]
- D: Organisation obscures the argument: no identifiable thesis placement, paragraphs without unity, repetition, absent transitions, material that does no work. [S4 D, S5 B-]
- F: No organising principle; a run of unconnected statements. [S4 F]
- Checklist for the reviewer, each checked at paragraph level: (1) where the thesis first appears; (2) is there a roadmap, and does the body match it; (3) one-sentence summary of each paragraph, does it serve the thesis; (4) premise order, does anything depend on something not yet argued; (5) transitions, is the logical relation stated or merely implied; (6) proportion, rough share of words on exposition, argument, strongest objection and reply; (7) conclusion, does it overreach. [own, built from S4, S7, H1 to H4]
- For experimental or theory-fiction work, replace thesis-first with "the formal logic of the piece is discernible and serves its declared aims" (see 3.4). [own]

### 2.4 Use of sources
- A: Secondary sources are relevant and authoritative and are engaged critically (used to extend, test or contest the essay's own argument, not decorate it); quotation and citation are accurate and consistent; the writer's voice stays dominant. [S2 sources and evidence, S1 evidence, S4 "accurately and thoughtfully uses other sources"]
- B: Relevant, credible sources used accurately and cited consistently; engagement mostly summary or agreement, or a few sources carry too much weight. [S2 milestone]
- C: Sources thin, uneven or dated; quotations dropped in without comment; citation errors; some paraphrase close enough to the original to read as patchwriting. [S2 benchmark, patchwriting guidance]
- D: Few or irrelevant sources, uncritical reliance, long undigested quotation, frequent citation errors, or borrowed phrasing without attribution. [own]
- F: No sources where required, fabricated or misattributed sources or quotations, or substantial unacknowledged use of others' words. [own]

### 2.5 Originality
- A: Offers an idea, distinction, counterexample, reading or application that goes beyond what the sources and course supplied, worked out deeply enough to show a firm grasp of the underlying principles; the reader learns something. [S3 A, S5 A, S6 A, S1 capstone position]
- B: Independent and competent but largely within received interpretations; an apt analogy or sharp distinction may appear without being fully developed. [S3 B+, S5 B and B+]
- C: Reproduces standard material and arguments with little contribution of its own; the writer's view is hard to tell from the sources'. [S5 C]
- D: Largely derivative; standard objections and replies restated with no sign of independent thought. [own]
- F: Entirely derivative or copied. [own]
- Note: judge originality relative to what was available to the student (S5), and never reward novelty that rests on misreading the text.

### 2.6 Mechanics
- A: Precise, economical, readable prose, essentially free of grammar, spelling, punctuation, typographical and citation-format errors; sentences use only the words their ideas require. [S4 A, S7 A, S2 capstone]
- B: Clear and effective; a few slips or small patches of padding that never impede meaning. [S4 B]
- C: Understandable, but errors, awkwardness or wordiness are conspicuous and sometimes cloud meaning; needs proofreading. [S4 C, S7 C "repairable without major rewriting"]
- D: Frequent errors or imprecise diction that regularly distract or impede; pervasive padding. [S2 benchmark, S4 D]
- F: Errors so pervasive that meaning is often unrecoverable. [own]
- Fairness note: grade whether meaning is clear, not whether idiom is native-like. See the detector-bias evidence in section 4.
- Design choice for the user: this draft puts concision and padding under Mechanics. Alternative is Structure (S5 treats padding as a structural fault).

### 2.7 Plus and minus, and the overall grade
- Use S5's logic, which maps cleanly onto six criteria: a plus means the work meets the band's descriptor fully and shows real strength of the band above (S5 B+); a minus means it meets the band but with a notable weakness of the band below (S5 B-). A- is excellence on most A criteria with one weaker (S5). [S5, own]
- The sources assign one overall grade by judgment and do not average. Decide whether the plugin averages six criteria or reports the six grades and lets the user judge. Averaging hides the "one conspicuous flaw caps the grade" pattern in S4. [own]

## 3. Philosophy-specific norms for rules.md

### 3.1 Charity and steelmanning
- Principle: restate and assess the strongest reasonable version of a position before criticising it. Sources: Rapoport's rules as given by Dennett, *Intuition Pumps* (2013), summary at https://openculture.com/2019/06/how-to-argue-with-kindness-and-care-4-rules-from-philosopher-daniel-dennett.html [V]: re-express the target so well they say "I wish I'd put it that way", list points of agreement, note what you learned, only then rebut. Also H3 "argue against the most cogent version of the objection" [V].
- Rule candidates: the reviewer checks whether objections are stated in a form their holder would accept; flags straw men; never marks down a student for steelmanning a view the student rejects.
- Limit (Skinner, "Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas", *History and Theory* 1969 [V]): charity can become the "mythology of coherence", forcing a consistent system on a thinker who is not consistent, or praising an author for "anticipating" a later view (prolepsis). So charity applies to arguments, not a licence to smooth over real tensions in a text. [own phrasing of Skinner]

### 3.2 Exposition versus evaluation
- Sources: H3 (serious exposition first, even when asked to evaluate) [V]; H2 (Part 1 explicate, Part 2 object) [V]; S3 B ("grasp of their content, without merely replicating them") [V].
- Rule candidates: (a) the reviewer should be able to tell, sentence by sentence, whose claim is on the page (author's, a commentator's, the student's); flag passages where voice slips. (b) Exposition that is only summary does not earn evaluative credit. (c) Evaluative verdicts ("clearly fails", "obviously wrong") inside exposition are flagged as premature. (d) The feedback itself must keep the same line: say what the essay does before saying what is wrong with it. [own]

### 3.3 Deliberate departures from a source's terminology
- Sources: stipulative-definition guidance (define key terms and use them consistently, H3 [V]; a stipulative definition cannot be wrong, only more or less useful [V]); Skinner on anachronism and prolepsis [V]; S2 genre conventions allowing departures that serve purpose [V].
- Rule candidates (all [own] unless tagged): a departure is legitimate if flagged, motivated and held steady. Penalise unmarked drift and equivocation, not departure itself. Distinguish three cases and treat them differently: (1) a misreading of the source term (Command of primary texts); (2) a flagged stipulation ("I use X to mean Y") (fine, check consistency); (3) an unflagged redefinition (ask for a flag). When it is unclear which case applies, the reviewer asks "is this deliberate?" as a query, and never silently "corrects" it. Do not impose a "standard" lexicon on writers who work with coined or repurposed vocabulary (common in post-structuralist and critical-theory writing). Foreign-language terms and translations: the edition or translator should be identifiable and the original term given where the argument turns on it.

### 3.4 Theory-fiction and experimental genres versus argumentative essays
- Honest finding: I found no validated rubric for assessing theory-fiction or experimental critical writing. The field's own scholarship says criteria are unsettled. What exists is adjacent:
  - S2 builds genre-relativity into a mainstream rubric (conventions belong to a kind of text) [V].
  - Newcastle SELLL creative-writing marking criteria: creative work is judged on originality of form, voice and technical control, paired with a critical reflection on process and craft in disciplinary vocabulary. https://www.ncl.ac.uk/mediav8/elll/files/SELLL%20Marking%20Criteria%20-%20Creative%20Writing.pdf [V]. Model: the piece plus a rationale.
  - Hanley (2019), "Thinking with Deleuze and Guattari: an exploration of writing as assemblage", *Educational Philosophy and Theory* 51(4), https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/620574 [V]: findings written in an experimental mode, with the experiment theorised and assessed "in a non-reductive way". Experimental academic writing is usually defended by self-theorisation, not by an external rubric.
  - Schroeder, Fox and Bizzell (eds.), *ALT DIS: Alternative Discourses and the Academy* (Boynton/Cook, 2002) [V]: argues for hybrid and alternative discourse in academic writing. Not a marking scheme.
  - Writing on creative-critical PhDs notes that it stays unclear what the contribution is and how it will be judged (NAWE case study https://www.nawe.co.uk/DB/wip-editions/articles/critical-approaches-to-creative-writing-a-case-study.html; *New Writing* https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14790726.2026.2615123; exact attribution of that sentence not verified).
- Rule candidates ([own], built on the above): (1) The writer declares a mode at submission (argumentative essay, hybrid, theory-fiction or other) plus two to three sentences of intent (what the piece is trying to do, and what would count as success). (2) The reviewer grades against the declared aims, not against thesis-first conventions. Structure becomes "the formal logic is discernible and serves the aims". (3) Non-negotiables hold in every mode: the piece is intelligible to its intended reader; claims it makes about real sources and texts are accurate; quotations are intact; no padding. (4) "Experimental" is not a defence for having no discernible claim or stance, and not a bar to the highest grade. (5) If mode is undeclared and the form is unconventional, ask once, and do not assume it is an error. (6) An open question for the user: do mode-specific descriptors for Structure and Argument go in `grade-bands.md`, or in a separate genre addendum?

### 3.5 Quotation integrity and glossing
- Integrity (Chicago Manual of Style Q&A, https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/qanda/data/faq/topics/Quotations.html [V]): fidelity to the original is paramount. Alterations are marked in square brackets; ellipses show omissions; *sic* marks an error preserved from the source; "emphasis added" marks the writer's italics; any unmarked deviation counts as an error. Do not let omissions change the sense.
- Glossing (writing-guide summaries, probably after Booth, Colomb and Williams, *The Craft of Research*; original not verified) [V]: quote when the exact words are the evidence, are from an authority, are strikingly original, or state a view you reject and want to state exactly. Otherwise paraphrase with a citation. Patchwriting (swapping synonyms into someone's sentences) is a form of plagiarism [V]. The writer's voice should dominate.
- Rule candidates ([own], built on the above): every quotation is explained (what it says, why it matters to the argument) in the writer's words; quotation that stands in for exposition is flagged; the tool never alters, trims or "fixes" a student's quotation in suggested text; a quotation cannot be checked without the text, so the reviewer marks it "cannot verify against the source" and never calls it inaccurate on recollection; suspected misquotation is raised as a query with page reference, not a verdict; fabricated or unverifiable quotations and citations are flagged for human check and never silently accepted.
- Other candidate non-negotiables ([own]): the reviewer distinguishes "I disagree" from "this is an error"; it names its confidence; it grades the draft in front of it and does not rewrite the student's argument for them.

## 4. Filler phrases: what the evidence says

### 4.1 What exists
- Word-level shifts are real at population scale. Kobak et al., "Delving into ChatGPT usage in academic writing through excess vocabulary" (later retitled; *Science Advances* 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016) [V] studied 14 million PubMed abstracts from 2010 to 2024 and found abrupt rises in "style words" after LLMs appeared. The ~900 excess words include verbs and adjectives like "delves", "showcasing", "underscores", "pivotal", "crucial", "intricate" (word examples partly [M]); a lower bound of at least 10% of 2024 abstracts processed with LLMs, up to 30% in some subcorpora.
- Juzek and Ward, "Why Does ChatGPT 'Delve' So Much?" (COLING 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11385) [V]: 21 focal words ("delve", "intricate", "underscore") overrepresented in science abstracts; explores RLHF and alignment as a cause. Causes are hypotheses.
- Wikipedia's editor guide "Signs of AI writing" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) [V] catalogues stock phrases: "stands as a testament", "plays a vital role", "underscores its importance", "rich cultural tapestry", "it's important to note", "no discussion would be complete without", and section-ending "In summary" or "Overall". Its own caveat [V]: these signs alone do not prove AI authorship, LLMs learned them from human writing, look for several signs together, and do not rely on detectors alone.
- Padding lists that predate LLMs: Purdue OWL conciseness (https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/conciseness/changing_phrases.html) [V]: "due to the fact that", "at this point in time", "for the purpose of", "in spite of the fact that"; Plain English Campaign A-Z (https://www.plainenglish.co.uk/the-a-z-of-alternative-words) [V]: "in view of the fact that", "at this moment in time", "the fact of the matter is", "at the end of the day", "in the final analysis"; Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946) on prefabricated phrases [V]; Sword on "zombie nouns" (nominalisations) [V].

### 4.2 These lists are NOT evidence of AI authorship, and detectors are unreliable
- Excess-word studies describe aggregate corpora, not individual texts. They cannot say who wrote one essay. Native or non-native writers, and edited or translated text, may use these words (Hern's theory that "delve" comes from Nigerian English via outsourced RLHF raters was widely repeated but is a speculation; see https://simonwillison.net/2024/Apr/18/delve [V]).
- Liang et al., "GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers", *Patterns* 2023 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819) [V]: seven detectors misclassified TOEFL essays by non-native writers as AI with a mean false-positive rate of 61.3%, and at least one detector flagged 97.8% of them; US eighth-grade essays were classified accurately. Explanation offered: detectors lean on low perplexity, which restricted vocabulary produces.
- Weber-Wulff et al., "Testing of detection tools for AI-generated text", *Int. J. for Educational Integrity* 2023 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15666) [V]: 14 tools; all below 80% accuracy; neither accurate nor reliable; false positives from 6 of 14; worse on paraphrased or machine-translated text.
- Sadasivan et al., "Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156) [V]: recursive paraphrasing defeats detectors, and spoofing can make human text look AI-written.
- OpenAI withdrew its own text classifier in July 2023 citing low accuracy [V, secondary: https://searchenginejournal.com/openai-shuts-down-flawed-ai-detector/492565]; at launch it flagged only about 26% of AI text and mislabelled about 9% of human text [M].
- Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin's AI detector in August 2023 [V]: a claimed 1% false-positive rate would have meant about 750 of 75,000 papers wrongly flagged; also bias and opacity. https://www.vanderbilt.edu/brightspace/2023/08/16/guidance-on-ai-detection-and-why-were-disabling-turnitins-ai-detector/
- Evidence moves. Al Ali et al., "Different Time, Different Language" (EACL 2026 SRW; https://aclanthology.org/2026.eacl-srw.20) [V]: in Czech, modern detectors from three families showed no systematic bias against non-native writers and did not rely on perplexity. So the 2023 bias finding may not hold for newer detectors or other languages, but it has not been refuted for English by this study, and nothing shows detectors are safe for high-stakes accusations.
- Russell, Karpinska and Iyyer (ACL 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.15654) [V]: five annotators who use LLMs often, by majority vote, misclassified 1 of 300 articles, and leaned on "AI vocabulary" plus formality, originality and clarity cues. This supports lexical clues as soft signals for experts in aggregate, and says nothing about the reliability of a keyword list on one essay.
- Conclusion: a word list can show padding or house style, but cannot prove who wrote anything. AI-written drafts can be free of these phrases and human drafts full of them.

### 4.3 Recommendations for rules.md and filler-phrases.md
- Frame `filler-phrases.md` as a quality aid: "padding or vacuous phrase, regardless of author". The plugin must never say or imply AI authorship, never cite detectors, and never lower a grade for resemblance to AI text. Suggest `rules.md` states this as a non-negotiable. [own]
- Matching: case-insensitive; skip quoted material and block quotes; report as "possible padding at line N", counted by density per 1,000 words so one use is not penalised; always a query, never an automatic deduction. [own]
- Keep out of the list: single "AI-sounding" words (delve, tapestry, intricate, pivotal, underscore, multifaceted). They are legitimate words, and flagging them hits non-native and careful writers hardest. If wanted, keep them in a separate optional "vocabulary watchlist" for variety, off by default. [own]
- Deliberately excluded because they are legitimate signposts: "in conclusion", "this essay will argue", "I will argue", "on the other hand", "overall". Pryor and others want roadmaps; first person is fine. Vague roadmaps ("this essay will discuss") are better caught by the Structure checklist than by grep. [own]
- The first three entries of the last group below ("many people believe that", "some would argue that", "it can be argued that") are padding only when no source or named opponent follows. Treat them as conditional queries. [own]
