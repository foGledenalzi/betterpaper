Date: 2026-10-06. Ten research lenses searched the literature for evidence that narrows or sharpens the plan's known limits; an independent verifier then checked that each cited source exists. **Provenance caveats, read first:**

- Existence checks: 91 source rows confirmed, 17 uncertain, 0 not found. Confirmation came from search-result text; arXiv, publisher and press hosts were blocked by the network proxy, so almost nothing was read in full. Several author lists, venues and figures were not seen. Treat every number below as a pointer to check, not a quotable result.

## Updates to each known limit

### 1. Calibration evidence rests on school-level essays

**Confidence:** medium

**What we now know.** Still true for the calibration and anchoring gains. The confirmed anchor or few-shot results are on school or L2 corpora: Choi et al. (anchor papers, secondary-school argumentative essays), RES and MADRAG (ASAP), and a 117-essay Chinese EFL study in which Claude few-shot was closer to human raters than zero-shot (0.46 to 0.38 logits). University-level essay evidence now exists, but none of it is postgraduate humanities. OpRaise (Cambridge-led): 761 undergraduate psychology essays at three UK universities, three frontier models including Claude, band match from 35% (Manchester Metropolitan) to 63% (Cambridge); press-level, report unread. Gaggioli et al.: 67 Italian-language psychology essays, five LLMs including Claude 3.5, human-LLM QWK low and non-significant. JMBE biology: 200 undergraduate essays, moderate alignment with the instructor, LLM spread generally narrower than the human's. Computers & Education: AI: 60 long essays by non-native higher-education students, five LLMs including Claude Sonnet 4. Yeadon et al. (physics): validity tracked how criterion-referenceable the task was more than which model graded. GradeLegal (university-level law, 27 LLMs, expert grades) is the best positive higher-education result, but it is law, not open-ended argument. We found no study of philosophy, critical-theory or history essays at any level.

**Remaining gap.** No postgraduate humanities study was found, and the search budget and blocked hosts mean 'none found' is not 'none exist'. Leads, existence unverified and not counted above: Lundgren (arXiv:2406.16510; reported as master's-level political science, 60 essays, best prompt 35% agreement, kappa 0.18) and a 2026 Frontiers public-health master's exam study (reported weighted kappa 0.718 for one model, 50.0% exact agreement). OpRaise per-model tables and the report itself are unseen.

**Sources.** OpRaise report/press release (Talmi-led, Cambridge, 2026); Gaggioli, Casaburi et al. arXiv:2508.02442; JMBE biology doi:10.1128/jmbe.00095-26; Computers & Education: AI S2666920X26000275; Yeadon et al. arXiv:2603.14732; GradeLegal arXiv:2605.21076; Choi, Tate, Warschauer, Assessing Writing 69:101053; Jang et al. RES arXiv:2509.14834; Keramati et al. MADRAG arXiv:2606.06754; Educ Inf Technol 30:25881-25908 (EFL, ChatGPT vs Claude)

### 2. No validation against human graders on graduate humanities

**Confidence:** medium

**What we now know.** Nothing confirmed validates an LLM grader against human graders on graduate humanities. The human-reliability lens returned no verified source at all, so we also have no confirmed human-human baseline for humanities. The nearest confirmed human comparisons are undergraduate (OpRaise, psychology), other disciplines (Gaggioli, JMBE, physics, law) or school-level (ASAP, EFL). For human behaviour, one RCT of 108 researchers found no significant evidence that re-reviewers anchored on their own earlier score (Liu et al., one null result with limited power). That does not cover a human confirming a grade the LLM proposed, which is the plugin's actual step.

**Remaining gap.** A small local validation is the only way to close this: human-human and plugin-human agreement on the same drafts. Leads, existence unverified: Lundgren (master's political science), the Frontiers public-health master's study, and the AQA marking-reliability review (reported as citing Hartog and Rhodes 1936: history honours script pair agreement from -0.41 to 0.85, mean 0.44, second-hand and old).

**Sources.** OpRaise report (2026); Gaggioli et al. arXiv:2508.02442; Liu et al., PLOS ONE e0301111; Yeadon et al. arXiv:2603.14732

### 3. Top-band unreliability and direction of error

**Confidence:** medium

**What we now know.** Confirmed evidence points to compression toward the middle, but the direction of error depends on the model and the essays. OpRaise (press-level): marks compressed toward the middle, best and worst essays marked least accurately, weak essays inflated and strong ones marked down. JMBE biology: LLM spread narrower than the instructor's. Computers & Education: AI: among five LLMs some showed score compression or systematic underestimation. Sunkavalli: judge severity differs by up to 219 points on a 0-1000 scale (school-level corpora), so no single 'lenient' or 'harsh' direction can be assumed. Mathew et al. (GPT and Llama): higher scores for short or underdeveloped essays and lower for longer essays with minor errors. Two results bear on the top of a revision path: weakening edits were detected less reliably than strengthening ones (CreditTrace-LLM: targeted points moved as expected in 75.7% vs 50.2% of comparisons), and Preiss and Yang found reviews matched human strengths more reliably than weaknesses.

**Remaining gap.** No confirmed measurement of top-band accuracy at postgraduate level or for Claude specifically. OpRaise per-model, per-band tables are unseen. Summaries of the length and vocabulary effect conflict (OpRaise: longer and richer language rewarded; Mathew et al.: longer essays with minor errors scored lower), so the sign of the length effect is unresolved.

**Sources.** OpRaise report/press release (2026); JMBE biology doi:10.1128/jmbe.00095-26; Computers & Education: AI S2666920X26000275; Sunkavalli arXiv:2608.29517; Mathew et al. arXiv:2603.23714; CreditTrace-LLM, Computers 15(9):571; Preiss and Yang arXiv:2609.01905

### 4. Modest exact-grade agreement and human disagreement

**Confidence:** low

**What we now know.** The LLM side is consistently modest in confirmed university-level work: OpRaise band match 35% to 63% by institution; Gaggioli et al. QWK low and non-significant, with within-model Kendall's W median below .30 across three replications; Mathew et al. report LLM-human agreement generally weaker than between humans (the explicit comparison was not seen). The human side is not measured for humanities: no confirmed human-human figure was found. Two confirmed results caution against reading disagreement as pure LLM error. Guerdan et al. show that when several ratings are defensible, forced-choice validation against one label can pick judges up to 31% worse. Sunkavalli's school-level corpora show 15-33% of the score range between LLM judges on ASAP versus about 1% between two trained humans, so trained humans can be far more consistent than LLMs on some tasks.

**Remaining gap.** No humanities human-human baseline, so we cannot say whether 35-63% band match is below or near the human ceiling. Leads, existence unverified: AQA review citing Hartog and Rhodes (1936), the Frontiers public-health study (50.0% exact for ChatGPT, 28.1% for Kimi, as reported), and the Cope et al. law study (reported correlations up to 0.93 with a detailed rubric, 0.66-0.80 without).

**Sources.** OpRaise report (2026); Gaggioli et al. arXiv:2508.02442; Mathew et al. arXiv:2603.23714; Guerdan et al. arXiv:2503.05965; Sunkavalli arXiv:2608.29517

### 5. Style, register and non-native bias

**Confidence:** low

**What we now know.** Style and length sensitivity is measured but mostly outside graduate essay grading. OpRaise (press-level) reports models were oversensitive to linguistic features (sentence density, vocabulary, length) regardless of argument quality. LLM-REVal found LLM-authored papers scored higher (6.2142 vs 5.9371) while blinded humans preferred the human-authored paper in 56.7% of 15 pairs (ML conference papers). In pairwise chat judging, the AlpacaEval README reports a 0.68 probability of preferring the longer output for GPT-4 against 0.64 for humans, and a length-control step cuts length gameability from about 21% to about 6%. A label study found the 'Claude' label raised scores and false labels shifted quality ratings by up to 12 points (blog posts). Judging the Judges found formatting style (markdown vs plain with identical content) was the largest bias in chat pairwise judging. Non-native evidence is only about AI-text detectors (more than half of TOEFL essays classed as AI-generated), not graders. A study of paraphrase sensitivity found LLM relevance labels vary unpredictably with simple paraphrases.

**Remaining gap.** No measured magnitude for register, non-native phrasing or markdown effects on rubric-based essay grading with Claude. Direction of the length effect conflicts across sources. Leads, existence unverified: Walsh and Ivan (arXiv:2603.25674; reported robust to padding and spelling errors, but penalised duplicated passages).

**Sources.** OpRaise report/press release (2026); Li et al. LLM-REVal arXiv:2510.12367; Dubois et al. arXiv:2404.04475 (README); Saraf et al. arXiv:2508.21164; Judging the Judges arXiv:2604.23178; Liang et al. arXiv:2304.02819; Thomas et al. SIGIR 2024; Mathew et al. arXiv:2603.23714

### 6. Leniency or severity varies by model and prompt

**Confidence:** medium

**What we now know.** Confirmed and the best-supported of the bias limits, though on school corpora. Sunkavalli (pre-registered preprint): 12 judges from 4 providers, 2,377 essays; judge severity spans 219 points on the 0-1000 ENEM scale; all five same-provider version contrasts shifted severity, by up to 133 points; correlation-type statistics cannot see this. In the Chinese EFL study the distance from human raters differed by model and prompt (logits: ChatGPT few-shot 0.10, ChatGPT zero-shot 0.31, Claude few-shot 0.38, Claude zero-shot 0.46). EvalHack found a four-LLM panel more lenient than human graders (MAE 1.897). Yeadon et al. found Claude Opus 4.5 slightly ahead of peers on blind structured physics questions (rho 0.690 vs 0.646 for GPT-5.2) but essay results were not seen. Jiao et al. weakly support Claude 3.5 Sonnet as having fewer rater effects among ten LLMs, with the authors cautioning against rankings.

**Remaining gap.** No Claude-specific severity ranking on essays; Sunkavalli's Claude models were not identified. Prompt effects on essay severity are shown only indirectly (few-shot vs zero-shot, paraphrase sensitivity), and not for graduate work.

**Sources.** Sunkavalli arXiv:2608.29517; Educ Inf Technol 30:25881-25908; EvalHack, Information 17(3):297; Yeadon et al. arXiv:2603.14732; Jiao et al., EM:IP doi:10.1111/emip.70018; Thomas et al. SIGIR 2024

### 7. Panel not shown more accurate than a single grader

**Confidence:** medium

**What we now know.** The evidence is split and the specific design is untested. Supportive: PoLL (three smaller models from three families beat a single GPT-4-class judge on human agreement, over seven times cheaper; QA and chat tasks); GradeLegal (ensembling raised agreement by up to 0.15 over the best member in expert-graded university law); ChatEval (distinct role prompts helped, identical role descriptions hurt). Challenging: Kohli (nine frontier LLMs from seven families carry about two effective votes; the best single judge matched or beat the panel; NLI data); Hossain et al. (mean pairwise error correlation 0.21; ten judges worth about 3.5 independent ones); Choi, Zhu and Li (majority voting accounts for most of the gain usually attributed to debate); Zhang et al. (debate often fails to beat self-consistency; model heterogeneity was the one consistent improver); Yao et al. (in essay scoring, majority voting gave no stronger agreement with humans than deterministic scoring); Frohn (ensemble size 1 to 7 gave no significant gain on maths conversations). Together this means five reviewers in fresh contexts should not be treated as five independent looks, especially if they share a model family.

**Remaining gap.** No study of specialist-persona panels from one model family on graduate humanities essays, so whether different briefs decorrelate errors is untested. RES (Claude QWK up 34.86% over plain prompting, ASAP) and MADRAG (exemplar retrieval plus roles, ASAP) are compared against plain prompting, not an anchored single grader, so they do not isolate a panel effect.

**Sources.** Verga et al. PoLL arXiv:2404.18796; GradeLegal arXiv:2605.21076; Chan et al. ChatEval arXiv:2308.07201; Kohli arXiv:2605.29800; Hossain et al. arXiv:2609.22512; Choi, Zhu, Li arXiv:2508.17536; Zhang et al. arXiv:2502.08788; Yao, Yang, Kuang AIME-Con 2026; Frohn arXiv:2604.26954; Jang et al. arXiv:2509.14834; Keramati et al. arXiv:2606.06754

### 8. Weighted average is a design choice

**Confidence:** low

**What we now know.** Confirmed evidence shows no aggregation rule is universally best, and none tests a weighted average of six criterion grades. PoLL used max voting for binary correctness and average pooling for 1-5 scores because a three-judge panel often has no clear majority on five-point scales. As reported in a snippet of GradeLegal, the best rule differed by model group (median for one, minimum for another); the verifier confirmed the ensemble gain but not that detail. Kohli reports established aggregation methods closed at most 11% of the gap to independent voting (snippet-level). Hossain et al. recommend using a small set of trusted examples to estimate shared errors and choose the voting method. LLM-Rubric calibrates multi-question rubric outputs to human judgments (RMS error below 0.5 on a 1-4 scale, about twice as good as uncalibrated, dialogue ratings), which is the closest published alternative to fixed weights.

**Remaining gap.** No test of weighted mean vs median, minimum or learned calibration on essays, and no validation of the six weights. Lead, existence unverified: Gursahaney et al. (arXiv:2608.00717), reported as finding that replacing default criterion weights with weights from 84 supervisors barely changed AI-human alignment (mean relative deviation 11.18% to 10.85%, not significant), which would suggest the weights matter less than the criterion-level judgments.

**Sources.** Verga et al. PoLL arXiv:2404.18796; GradeLegal arXiv:2605.21076; Kohli arXiv:2605.29800; Hossain et al. arXiv:2609.22512; Hashemi et al. LLM-Rubric, ACL 2024

### 9. Integrity cap and quotation checking untested

**Confidence:** low

**What we now know.** Still untested for quotations. Confirmed work measures fabricated or corrupted reference metadata and URLs, not quotation text: Walters and Wilder (42 topics, 636 citations; 55% of GPT-3.5 and 18% of GPT-4 citations fabricated; among real ones 43% and 24% had substantive errors); Linardon et al. (35 of 176 GPT-4o citations, 19.9%, fabricated); Rao et al. (3-13% hallucinated URLs, 5-18% non-resolving; a liveness check plus self-correction cut non-resolving URLs 6-79x to under 1%, which proves a link exists, not that a quote is verbatim). Verifier design lessons: HALLMARK (2,526 BibTeX entries, 14 hallucination types) found agentic lookups raise recall but also false positives, that the false-positive rate decides deployability at realistic base rates, and that LLMs over-flag papers after their training cutoff. CiteCheck uses a retrieve-and-compare design with Exact/Minor/Major labels (982-citation physics benchmark, 88.7 macro-F1). AttriBench (11 LLMs) found quote attribution from memory remains hard for frontier models. LePhantomCite includes injected verbatim misquotes among its error types.

**Remaining gap.** No measured error rate for verbatim quotation fidelity, none for deterministic matching against supplied source text, and none on the false-positive causes that matter in humanities (editions, translations, ellipses, bracketed alterations, OCR, paraphrase flagged as quotation). The reading that false positives are the main risk for a cap is the lens's inference from HALLMARK, not a finding on quotations. LePhantomCite per-category figures were inconsistent across snippets and should not be quoted.

**Sources.** Walters and Wilder, Scientific Reports 2023; Linardon et al., JMIR Ment Health 2025; Rao, Wong, Callison-Burch arXiv:2604.03173; Reizinger and Brendel HALLMARK arXiv:2607.18360; Khajavi et al. CiteCheck arXiv:2605.27700; Berman et al. AttriBench arXiv:2604.05224; Liu, Stammbach, Henderson LePhantomCite arXiv:2606.21155

### 10. Run-to-run variation and uncalibrated confidence

**Confidence:** medium

**What we now know.** Run-to-run stability is model-, version- and task-dependent, and confidence signals discriminate only modestly. Gaggioli et al.: weak within-model reliability over three replications (median Kendall's W below .30). Computers & Education: AI: some models near-perfectly reproducible, others inconsistent; one snippet, attributed to this paper only by matching the model list, gave Claude Sonnet 4 mean inter-run r of about 0.55 (unconfirmed). Claude Haiku 4.5 reached ICC 0.977 with 94% identical scores at temperature 0, but on a non-essay alignment-scoring task. Rating Roulette: low self-agreement for open-weight judges. Yao et al.: LLM essay scorers were overconfident and highly consistent, and majority voting did not beat deterministic scoring. Ferrer et al.: self-reported confidence was best calibrated (average ECE 0.166 vs 0.229 for self-consistency) but the best model averaged AUC 0.668. CHiL(L)Grader: accepted predictions had QWK 0.347 higher than rejected ones (trained grader, short answers). Gupta and Kumar: conformal set size covered nearly the whole scale on subjective criteria (about 4.9 of 5 for fluency and consistency). Karim et al.: conformal coverage held at 90% on ASAP, TOEFL11 and Cambridge-FCE, but with fine-tuned models and labelled calibration data. Hossain et al.: agreement overstates evidence because judges' errors are correlated.

**Remaining gap.** No test of a categorical confidence note against human error for Claude on essays, none on a calibration set as small as a handful of human-confirmed drafts, and none per criterion. Confidence thresholds fitted to one model version may drift with the severity shifts in Sunkavalli. Sampling settings may not be controllable in a plugin harness.

**Sources.** Gaggioli et al. arXiv:2508.02442; Computers & Education: AI S2666920X26000275; Schreiber and Goldstein arXiv:2605.12199; Haldar and Hockenmaier arXiv:2510.27106; Yao, Yang, Kuang AIME-Con 2026; Ferrer et al. arXiv:2603.29559; Raikote et al. arXiv:2603.11957; Gupta and Kumar arXiv:2604.15302; Karim et al. arXiv:2509.15926; Hossain et al. arXiv:2609.22512; Sunkavalli arXiv:2608.29517

### 11. Feedback without rewriting not shown to help

**Confidence:** low

**What we now know.** No verified evidence either way. The feedback-effects lens returned no confirmed source, so nothing here shows that diagnostic, rewriting-free feedback improves revision or transferable skill at any level. Indirect confirmed evidence is cautionary and about LLM evaluators, not students: iterative refinement against an LLM evaluator's ratings raised the ratings while human-judged quality stagnated or fell, more so with shared context (Pan et al.); papers revised against LLM reviews gained LLM-reviewer score (5.79 to 6.09 and 5.66 to 6.02) while blinded humans preferred human-authored papers in 56.7% of 15 pairs (LLM-REVal); LLM grades were consistent with the feedback the same LLM wrote (Mathew et al.), so grade and comments are not independent checks.

**Remaining gap.** Whether rewriting-free diagnostic comments help graduate humanities writers, whether comment specificity or length matters, and whether showing a grade beside comments reduces uptake are all unevidenced here. The lens could only recall candidate studies from memory (for example Fan et al. 2025 on 'metacognitive laziness', Bastani et al. 2025 in PNAS, Lipnevich and Smith 2009); none was seen, so none is counted.

**Sources.** Pan et al. arXiv:2407.04549; Li et al. LLM-REVal arXiv:2510.12367; Mathew et al. arXiv:2603.23714

### 12. Fix-first ordering is a judgement call

**Confidence:** low

**What we now know.** No confirmed study tests ordering or prioritisation of feedback, so this remains a judgement call. The only indirect confirmed signals concern whether weaknesses are found reliably: Preiss and Yang found LLM reviews matched human strengths and upgrade points more reliably than weaknesses and downgrade points, and CreditTrace-LLM found weakening edits registered less reliably than strengthening ones. If weaknesses are the less reliably detected side, a fix-first list is built on the weaker half of the signal.

**Remaining gap.** No evidence on ranked-few versus exhaustive comments, or on severity-ordered versus rubric-ordered feedback, for graduate writers. Classic feedback literature (for example Hattie and Timperley) was not read in this run.

**Sources.** Preiss and Yang arXiv:2609.01905; CreditTrace-LLM, Computers 15(9):571

### 13. Anchors from earlier drafts may pull grades

**Confidence:** medium

**What we now know.** The risk is real in several confirmed settings, but nobody has tested successive drafts of one postgraduate humanities essay. Kapetanovic et al.: prior scores supplied as metadata anchored judgments in 7 of 8 models on 20 fixed texts (absolute d up to 0.71); neither chain-of-thought nor an instruction to disregard the metadata removed the total effect; on categorical data anchoring blocked 48% of error corrections and flipped 10.18% of correct judgments (prior scores there were random, so this measures susceptibility, not the net effect of an informative anchor). GradeDrift-LLM: student-history metadata moved scores despite an instruction to ignore it, but across 39,997 paired comparisons 83.92% showed no drift, 9.40% drifted up and 6.68% down. Suggestible Judges (preprint): a Claude Opus 4.7 judge conformed to supplied labels (random labels moved verdicts by +6.5 points) while GPT-5.5 resisted. AMEL: fresh context per item defends against accumulated conversation history, but it does not test a prior grade the pipeline itself injects. Evaluating Scoring Bias: a score shown with a reference answer substantially changes the score distribution. Few-shot example choice and order bias GPT-era scorers (majority-label and recency bias). Counter-evidence: an accurate anchor can help (Preiss and Yang, 98 research outputs; oracle scores improved accuracy and the models did not simply copy them), and anchors spanning the full score range beat partial anchoring (Choi et al., secondary school). Chen et al. found judges highly stable under neutral edits (0.945) but responsive to only 0.319 of construct-changing edits, so small draft-to-draft changes may not register.

**Remaining gap.** No test with Claude on essays, none with a stale-but-informative earlier grade, none showing whether human-confirmed anchors anchor less than unverified ones, and none showing that fresh-context reviewers or a deterministic adjudicator reduce anchoring. The Kapetanovic model list (any Claude?) is unseen. Severity shifts across model versions mean an anchor graded under one version is on a different scale from later grades.

**Sources.** Kapetanovic et al. arXiv:2608.25869; GradeDrift-LLM, MAKE 8(8):252; Suggestible Judges, Research Square rs-10688342; Temkit AMEL arXiv:2605.22714; Li et al. arXiv:2506.22316; Yoshida et al. arXiv:2411.18924; Preiss and Yang arXiv:2609.01905; Choi, Tate, Warschauer 2026; Chen et al. arXiv:2608.24419; Sunkavalli arXiv:2608.29517

### 14. Prompt injection

**Confidence:** medium

**What we now know.** Injection into graded documents works against some models and a panel is not protection by itself. Wharton Prompting Science Report 5 (about 40,000 grading trials; verifier confirmed only the general pattern): negligible effects for most frontier models, Gemini 3 Pro meaningfully vulnerable to verbose injections at the beginning or middle of the longer-paper corpus, GPT-4o mini scores up about 20 percentage points; the lens summary also reports only minimal effects for Claude Opus 4.5, which the verifier did not separately confirm. EvalHack: a four-LLM panel's median grade went from 7.0 to 10.0 under both a visible suffix and a Unicode-control-character injection. Li et al.: simple universal injections raise scores while existing defences are deployed. Raina et al.: judges are more susceptible in absolute scoring than comparative assessment. Adversarial Attacks on LLM-as-a-Judge: up to 73.8% attack success, smaller models more vulnerable, multi-model committees and comparative scoring recommended (the committee figure of 10.2-19.3% appeared only in a summary that blended papers). Cai (Scientific Reports 2026): rubric-aware attacks reached success rates of 0.73-0.82 across four benchmarks (target models unseen). Nasr et al.: adaptive attacks bypassed 12 defences with success above 90% in most cases (agent and safety settings). Spotlighting cut non-adaptive attack success from over 50% to under 2% on GPT-family models. A trade-press report of a Gray Swan benchmark gave Opus 4.5 4.7% success for one strong attack, 33.6% at ten attempts and 63% at 100 (tool-use agents, secondhand). Hidden prompts appear in real manuscripts (18 arXiv manuscripts, July 2025, Lin).

**Remaining gap.** No study of delimiters, instruction hierarchy or sanitisation on essay grading with Claude, none on graduate humanities, none on injection through legitimately quoted imperative text, and none on false positives of injection detectors. The Wharton report's corpora and exact per-model numbers are unseen, and two snippets of it could not be reconciled. A student iterating over successive drafts gets repeated attempts, which the single-attempt figures understate. Lead, existence unverified: Kaneko (arXiv:2601.06884), reported as showing semantics-preserving paraphrase search can raise LLM reviewer scores with no instruction in the text.

**Sources.** Wanjura et al., Prompting Science Report 5 (SSRN 6510758); EvalHack, Information 17(3):297; Li et al. arXiv:2606.03090; Raina, Liusie, Gales arXiv:2402.14016; arXiv:2504.18333; Cai, Scientific Reports 16 (2026); Nasr, Carlini et al. arXiv:2510.09023; Hines et al. arXiv:2403.14720; The Decoder report of Gray Swan benchmark; Lin arXiv:2507.06185; Yamamura and Ganguli arXiv:2412.15275; Shi et al. arXiv:2403.17710; Kohli arXiv:2605.29800

## New limits found

1. Model-version drift: grades from different model versions are not on one scale. Sunkavalli found all five same-provider version contrasts shifted severity (up to 133 points on a 0-1000 scale; school-level corpora, preprint). A human-confirmed anchor, and a grade for draft 1, are tied to the model version that produced the paired judgements, so draft-to-draft comparison across a model change is unsafe without recalibration.
2. Responsiveness, not just reliability: judges can be stable yet insensitive to real change. Chen et al. report invariance 0.945 against construct sensitivity 0.319 at matched invariance, and CreditTrace-LLM found weakening edits registered less reliably than strengthening ones (75.7% vs 50.2% of targeted points moving as expected). A small or zero grade change between drafts may mean the grader missed the revision, and a worse revision may not be marked down.
3. Correlated errors inside the panel: about two effective votes from nine judges (Kohli) and mean pairwise error correlation 0.21 with ten judges worth about 3.5 independent ones (Hossain et al.). Unanimity among reviewers is weaker evidence of correctness than it looks, which affects how the categorical confidence note should be worded. Neither study tested persona-specialised reviewers.
4. Rating indeterminacy: where several grades are defensible, validating against one human-confirmed grade biases both the choice of judge and the apparent error (Guerdan et al., up to 31% worse judges selected). Human-confirmed anchors are a noisy gold standard in humanities, and the human-confirmation step may itself be anchored on the grade the LLM proposed; the one human RCT we found tested only anchoring on one's own earlier score.
5. Feedback-loop reward hacking: if students revise with an LLM that sees the grader's report and are then re-graded by the same model family, the grade can rise without human-judged quality rising (Pan et al.; LLM-REVal pattern). The grade can become a target.
6. Anchor presentation effects: showing a score alongside a reference changes the score distribution (Evaluating Scoring Bias), and few-shot example choice and order bias GPT-era scorers (majority-label and recency bias). Which anchors are shown, in what order, and whether grades are visible are all uncontrolled variables for Claude.
7. Source or identity cues can move ratings: in a blog-post study the 'Claude' label raised scores and false labels shifted quality ratings by up to 12 points, and in a legal-adjudication preprint random labels moved a Claude judge by +6.5 points. Telling reviewers an essay was AI-assisted, or naming its source, may shift grades.
8. False positives of the integrity cap: HALLMARK's base-rate argument suggests that when a flag fires on honest work, the false-positive rate, not recall, decides usability. For quotations, 'unverifiable' (source not supplied, different edition, translation, ellipsis) is not 'fabricated', and no source measured how often honest essays would be capped. This is an inference, not a finding.
9. Hidden-text injection that graders may not announce: the lens summary of the Wharton report says models almost never verbalised that they had detected an injection (not independently verified), and EvalHack's Unicode-control-character variant moved a four-LLM panel's median to the maximum. A grader's silence is not evidence that no injection occurred, so ingestion needs its own checks.
10. Review limit: of 108 source rows returned by eight lenses (two further lenses, human reliability and feedback effects, returned none), 91 were confirmed, 17 uncertain and 0 not-found, so none were discarded. 6 of the 17 uncertain rows were confirmed under another lens and are used; the other 11 (Lundgren, the Cardiff/Melbourne bioscience study, the Frontiers public-health study, Cope et al., Gursahaney et al., the MDPI three-course study, the AQA/Hartog and Rhodes review, the 2512.14561 synthesis, Favero et al., Walsh and Ivan, Kaneko) are leads only. Confirmation was from search-result text; apart from README, abstract-page and documentation fetches, no confirmed source was read in full, and several author lists and figures were not seen. Treat every number as a pointer to check, not a quotable result.

## Plan changes suggested (adopted as A44-A53 in DECISIONS.md)

1. Log model ID, prompt hash and run date with every grade, and store the model version beside each human-confirmed anchor. When the version changes, show a notice and ask for a blind re-grade of one earlier draft before comparing grades across the change (Sunkavalli; version-driven severity shifts).
2. Add an optional blind-versus-anchored check: grade a draft once without earlier grades and once with them, set the human-check flag if the two differ by a margin you choose, and report both. Include anchors that span the grade range and randomise anchor order across reviewers (Kapetanovic; GradeDrift-LLM; Choi et al.; few-shot order bias).
3. Reword the confidence note so it does not equate reviewer agreement with independent confirmation. Base it on criterion dispersion, the blind-versus-anchored gap, unverified quotations and a model-version change, vary the reviewers' briefs (and the model where feasible), and describe it publicly as a triage heuristic (Kohli; Hossain et al.; ChatEval).
4. Quotation check: use three outcomes, matched, near-match with the difference named (edition, translation, ellipsis, bracketed change), and not checked or unmatched. Apply the integrity cap only to an unmatched quotation when the source text was supplied, and let 'source not supplied' produce a 'not checked' line, never a cap. Use graded labels like CiteCheck's Exact/Minor/Major. This is a design inference from HALLMARK's base-rate argument, not a tested rule.
5. Ingestion hygiene: strip or flag zero-width and bidirectional control characters, compare visible text with extracted text for PDF and docx input, place the essay in a delimited, lower-privilege block, and show 'injection-like text found' in the report. Do not rely on the grader to announce an injection (Wharton lens summary, unverified; EvalHack; Lin).
6. Before release, red-team the real plugin with simple and rubric-aware injections, Unicode-hidden text and repeated attempts across successive drafts, plus a set of essays that legitimately quote imperative text to count false positives. Publish results with the model version (Nasr et al.; Cai; Gray Swan report).
7. Before release, run a few human-graded drafts several times and publish the observed spread instead of asserting stability. Include perturbations: length padding, plain-register paraphrase, markdown stripped, anchor order permuted. Publish effect sizes (Rating Roulette; Computers & Education: AI; OpRaise; AlpacaEval).
8. Treat small draft-to-draft grade changes as 'no reliable change' unless criterion-level evidence supports them, and add a revision-sensitivity self-test: strengthen and weaken a paragraph of a test essay and check the grade moves the right way (Chen et al.; CreditTrace-LLM).
9. Collect a small double-marked set of drafts and report human-human and plugin-human agreement on the same drafts, per criterion, allowing that more than one grade may be defensible (Guerdan et al.; Sunkavalli's human-pair spread).
10. State that the six weights are a design choice, publish them, and run a one-off sensitivity check against equal weights, median and minimum on the validation set. Keep the integrity cap separate and visible in the report (PoLL; GradeLegal; Hossain et al.).
11. Add one line to the report and README: the grade is not a target to optimise, and rewriting a draft with an LLM that has seen the report may raise the grade without improving quality (Pan et al.; LLM-REVal).
12. Update the public limits text: the closest published evidence is undergraduate or school-level; grades compress toward the middle and the top and bottom bands are least reliable; the plugin is unvalidated on graduate humanities; anchoring on earlier grades and reviewer correlation are acknowledged risks; feedback effects are unevidenced.

## Claims to avoid

1. Any statement that calibration, few-shot or anchor gains hold for graduate humanities. The confirmed gains are on ASAP, EFL, secondary-school or other-discipline data.
2. Any statement that five reviewers are five independent checks, or that reviewer agreement is confidence. The evidence is about two effective votes from nine judges and 3.5 from ten (Kohli; Hossain et al.), and nobody tested same-family persona panels.
3. That fresh contexts remove anchoring. AMEL covers accumulated conversation history only; Kapetanovic shows a prior score placed in the prompt anchors 7 of 8 models, and chain-of-thought and a disregard warning did not remove the total effect.
4. That the deterministic adjudicator or the integrity cap makes the plugin injection-proof. EvalHack moved a four-LLM panel to the maximum, and the adjudicator only aggregates what reviewers output.
5. That Claude is robust to injection, stable run to run, lenient or severe. Claude-specific evidence is thin, snippet-level and mixed (Opus 4.5 minimal effect in one report summary; 4.7% single-attempt but 33.6% at ten attempts in a trade-press report of a different task; Sonnet 4 r about 0.55 attributed only by model-list match; Haiku 4.5 ICC 0.977 on a non-essay task).
6. Any error rate for quotation verification, or any claim that deterministic quote matching is accurate. The measured rates concern reference metadata and URLs, and the LePhantomCite per-category figures were inconsistent across snippets.
7. That the confidence note or human-check flag reliably marks larger errors. The best discrimination figure seen is an average AUC of 0.668, conformal guarantees need labelled calibration data and mostly fine-tuned models, and conformal sets cover nearly the whole scale on subjective criteria.
8. Numbers from existence-unverified leads: correlations up to 0.93 with law professors, weighted kappa 0.72 for master's essays, kappa 0.18 for master's political science, Hartog and Rhodes agreement figures, the thesis-weights result.
9. That LLMs are generally lenient or generally harsh. Direction varies by model, version and essay (Sunkavalli; Computers & Education: AI; OpRaise). The sign of the length effect also conflicts across sources.
10. That a draft-to-draft grade change reflects real improvement or decline. Judges can be stable yet insensitive to construct change (Chen et al.) and weakening is detected less reliably than strengthening (CreditTrace-LLM).
11. That the weighted average, or its six weights, is validated or better than alternatives. No study tests it.
12. That rewriting-free, diagnostic or fix-first feedback improves revision or learning. No confirmed source tests it, and the nearest evidence on LLM revision loops is cautionary.
13. That human-confirmed grades are a gold standard in humanities. No humanities human-human reliability figure was confirmed, and rating indeterminacy can bias validation against a single label.
14. Any non-native or register penalty in LLM graders, citing the GPT-detector paper. That paper measures AI-text detectors, not graders.
15. Quoting OpRaise as if read in full. Evidence is press-level, the report was not opened, the headline says 'mark' not 'grade', per-model results are unseen, and the release date and model versions are reported inconsistently.
16. Describing the 2026 preprints as peer-reviewed (Kapetanovic is a CIKM '26 paper per the abstract page; Kohli, Hossain, Sunkavalli, Chen and GradeLegal are preprints; Sunkavalli is reported as under review).
17. Saying 'no study exists'. Say 'we found none'; searches were budget-limited and most publisher hosts were blocked.
