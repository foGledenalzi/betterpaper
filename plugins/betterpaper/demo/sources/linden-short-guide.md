# A Short Guide to Karvale's Tessel-Glass

Petra Linden. Last updated 2030-09-12. Address: https://guides.example/karvale

---

Ismund Karvale's treatise of 1926, read in English in Dorothea Pelling's version of 1934, describes a small instrument and makes a large claim about it. The instrument is a tapering glass tube part filled with grit and marked along its side; the claim is that what it shows is not what it seems to show.

Keep three terms apart. A mark is a line on the scale, and Karvale treats each mark as a question put to whatever the glass is held against. A reading is the level at which the grit comes to rest. A tally is what the scale was cut to ask, and it is not a count of anything the stack does. In short, a reading is the glass's reply to a question it did not choose.

Later writers have carried the point beyond the glass. As Weir-Anand puts it, "every scale is a standing question." On this view an instrument is never neutral, and its maker's choices are already present in every reading it gives.

Two cautions. The treatise separates the settling of the grit from the settling of the observer, and it says that the lowest part of the scale is hard to read. The revised translation of 1971 rewords some sentences, though the sections keep their numbers.
