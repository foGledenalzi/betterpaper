# On the Reading of the Tessel-Glass

Ismund Karvale. Translated by Dorothea Pelling. First edition, Brennock & Vale, 1934, 64 pages. This file is the complete text of the invented first edition, sections 1 to 12; a marker such as [p. 9] gives the page of the passage that follows it.

## Section 1. The glass

[p. 9] The tessel-glass is a tapered tube of glass, part filled with fine grit and cut along its length with a scale of marks. It is held against a stack of laid tesserae, and the grit stirs as the stack leans.

[p. 10] When the grit has come to rest, the level it has reached on the scale is the reading. Nothing else about the glass is read.

## Section 2. The mark

[p. 11] Each mark asks a question of the stack; a reading only answers it. A reading answers only the question that the mark puts.

## Section 3. The tally

[p. 14] A tally is not a count. The tally is whatever the scale was cut to ask, and it belongs to the maker.

[p. 15] A reading is a tally of what the scale asks and not of what the stack does.

[p. 16] The tally is kept by the scale and read by the eye.

[p. 17] Whoever takes the tally for a count has handed the stack a part that it never played.

## Section 4. The settle

[p. 19] The grit settles of itself, and the observer settles on the nearer mark. These are two settlings, and the second depends on the first.

[p. 20] A glass is tapped once and watched again; if the level has moved, the grit had not truly come to rest.

## Section 5. The foot

[p. 23] Even when the grit has settled twice, a reading taken at the foot of the glass may still be mistaken, because the thick grit hides the lowest mark.

## Section 6. The known stack

[p. 28] Put the glass to a stack whose lean is known, and it will still give the tally its scale was cut to take, and no other.

[p. 29] The makers' trial shows only that the glass is sound.

## Section 7. The dark

[p. 33] In the dark the observer reads by touch, and the touch is trained to the marks, not to the stack.

## Section 8. Glasses alike

[p. 39] Two glasses cut alike will give two readings alike on one stack, and the agreement is made by the cutting, not found in the stack.

[p. 40] Makers who boast of agreement are boasting of their own hands.

## Section 9. The false zero

[p. 44] Where the grit lies level with no mark, the glass asks nothing and tells nothing.

[p. 45] The eye is eager to read a nothing as a number, and I call the number so read the false zero.

## Section 10. The maker's question

[p. 49] Every glass carries its maker's question, and no glass can carry another's.

## Section 11. The finer scale

[p. 55] A scale cut finer asks more of the stack, and the stack can answer less.

[p. 56] Every refinement of the scale is paid for in the confidence of the reading.

## Section 12. Honesty

[p. 60] A glass is honest when it is read as the question it was cut to ask.

[p. 64] Here the account of the glass ends.
