# Reading and Report in the Tessel-Glass: A Short Version

Few instruments are as plain as the tessel-glass, and few are so readily misdescribed. It is a tapering tube of glass, half filled with grit and scored along its side with a scale of marks; held against a stack, it lets the grit come to rest, and the level reached is called the reading. Most readers assume that this level simply reports the lean of the stack, as a dial would. This essay argues that a reading is a tally, an answer fixed by the question its scale asks, not a report of the lean it indicates, and that the claim survives the objection from calibration.

A glass at rest shows what the account adds to the ordinary view. Once the grit has stopped, the level reached is the count the glass keeps, and a view on which the scale merely records the lean cannot say why two scales give two counts from one stack, and must treat the difference as an error. A view that puts convention first can say it, but at the price of making the stack idle. In its favour, the tally account reaches the false zero, where the scale asks nothing, and explains that case without a special rule: no question was put, so no answer is owed.

In the end the tessel-glass is honest only on the terms of its maker. It speaks when it is asked, and it answers the question cut into its scale and no other. A reading is therefore not a report of the stack, and to demand one is to demand what no scale promised. That is a modest result: the scale is to be read before the stack is blamed. The essay has tried to show that this survives the objection from calibration, since checking a glass against a known lean refines its question without changing whose question it is. The same holds of every dial in the harbour.
