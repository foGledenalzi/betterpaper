# What the Flame Reads: The Gallery Lamp at Orrimar Head as a Gauge of Wind

The gallery lamp at Orrimar Head is a plain oil lamp, yet its keepers have long treated the height of its flame as a gauge of the wind. This essay argues that flame height tracks wind speed reliably between about fifteen and forty knots, that it fails outside that band, and that the failures at the edges explain why the keepers confirm the flame by ear. By flame height I mean the visible extent of the flame against the etched collar of the chimney, read to the nearest mark.

The evidence is the keepers' daybook for the winter of 2029, which sets a flame reading beside an anemometer reading taken from the same gallery at each of the four night watches.[^1] Of 212 paired entries, 148 fall inside the band. In these the flame stands one collar mark lower for each rise of about five knots, with a correlation of 0.91. Below fifteen knots the flame stays at the top mark whatever the wind does, and above forty it flattens and gutters, so that the readings carry no information.

The strongest objection is that oil level and wick trim, not wind, set the height of a flame, so that a falling reservoir would be mistaken for a rising gale. The objection rests on the premise that these causes vary enough across a night to produce the range observed. The daybook shows that they do not. The reservoir was filled to the collar at dusk, and its fall by dawn was measured at eight millimetres, which on the keepers' own bench test moves the flame by less than half a mark. The wick was trimmed at every watch. Across the 148 entries the largest spread in flame height at constant wind was one mark, while the spread produced by wind was five.

A second worry is that the gauge was never independent of the anemometer, since the keepers read both. In 126 of the 148 entries, however, the flame was recorded first, before the anemometer was consulted, and the correlation in those entries is the same to two places.

The flame is therefore a sound gauge in the middle of its range and an unsound one at its ends. The keepers' habit of listening for the wind at the edges is a reasonable answer to a known limit of the instrument, and not a lapse of trust in it.

[^1]: *Daybook of the Orrimar Head Light, 2029-30*, Harbour Office papers, fols. 12-61.
