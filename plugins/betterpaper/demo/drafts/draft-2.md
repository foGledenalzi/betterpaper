# What the Glass Answers: Reading and Report in Karvale's Tessel-Glass

Few instruments are as plain as the tessel-glass, and few are so readily misdescribed. Karvale introduces it in a single sentence: "The tessel-glass is a tapered tube of glass, part filled with fine grit and cut along its length with a scale of marks"[^1]. Most readers assume that the level of the grit reports the lean of the stack. This essay argues that a reading is a tally, an answer fixed by the question its scale asks, not a report of the lean it indicates, and that the claim survives the objection from calibration.

Karvale's account begins with a grammar of question and answer: "Each mark asks a question of the stack; a reading only answers it"[^2]. The mark fixes what a reading answers, since the question is cut before the grit moves, though each of the marks ask something different of the stack. The central term is the tally, which Karvale sets against a count: "A tally is not a count"[^3]. In my usage the word names a tally, or count, of what the stack does under that question, and so neither of the readings are a mere report of the lean of the stack.

An obvious objection comes from calibration. Makers test each glass against stacks of known lean, and a calibrated glass simply reports the lean, since the glass's scale has been checked against it and found to follow it across the whole length of its marks. Karvale puts the point briefly: "every scale is a standing question"[^4]. His reply to the objection is shorter still: "A reading is a tally of what the scale asks and not of what the stack does"[^5]. The objection therefore fails, and calibration need not detain the argument.

Consider a glass read in the dark. Karvale observes that "In the dark the observer reads by touch, and the touch is trained to the marks, not to the stack"[^6]. A surveyor reads the glass by touch in a dark shed, and her finger, not the lean of the stack, decides which mark the grit has reached. In this sense the observer settles the reading: the hand chooses the mark that the grit is taken to have touched, and the same holds of a glass no one has ever calibrated. A glass that has never met a known stack still has its scale, and its scale still asks. Calibration is therefore not what makes a reading a tally: the tally is there before any stack is met, whatever the glass has or has not been tested against.

Agreement between glasses has a different source from the one usually supposed. Two glasses cut alike give two readings alike on a single stack, and the agreement is made by the cutting, not found in the stack. Karvale adds that "A scale cut finer asks more of the stack, and the stack can answer less"[^7]. It follows that no reading can be wrong. Each reading is the right answer to the question its own scale put, and a glass read at the foot of its scale answers as faithfully as any other, whatever its age or make.

A glass at rest shows what the account adds to the ordinary view. The glass settles, the reader waits. What the reader then takes away is the count the glass keeps, and a view on which the scale merely records the lean cannot say why two scales should give two counts from one stack. A "convention-first" reading can say it, since it treats each scale as fixed by custom before any stack is met. On the tally account each reading is the answer to its own scale's question and to no other. Nothing in the stack decides which question that is.

In the end the tessel-glass is honest only on the terms of its maker. Karvale writes that "Every glass carries its maker's question, and no glass can carry another's"[^8], and that "A glass is honest when it is read as the question it was cut to ask"[^9]. A reading is not a report of the stack, and to ask it for one is to ask for what the scale never promised, however carefully the glass was made. The same holds of every dial in the harbour.
