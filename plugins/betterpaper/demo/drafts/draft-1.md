# What the Glass Answers: Reading and Report in Karvale's Tessel-Glass

Few instruments are as plain as the tessel-glass, and few are so readily misdescribed. Karvale introduces it in a single sentence: "The tessel-glass is a tapered tube of glass, part filled with fine grit and cut along its length with a scale of marks"[^1]. Most readers assume that the level of the grit reports the lean of the stack. This essay argues that a reading is a tally, an answer fixed by the question its scale asks, not a report of the lean it indicates, and that the claim survives the objection from calibration.

Karvale's account begins with a grammar of question and answer: "Each mark asks a question of the stack; a reading only answers it"[^2]. Since each of the marks ask something different, a reading fixes what the mark asks: the level of the grit singles out the question that was put. The central term is the tally, which Karvale sets against a count: "A tally is not a count"[^3]. I take a tally, that is, a count of what the stack does, to be the glass's output, and the scale and the grit remains its whole equipment.

An obvious objection comes from calibration. Makers test each glass against stacks of known lean, and a calibrated glass simply reports the lean, since the glasses scale has been checked against it and found to follow it across the whole length of its marks. Karvale replies in one line: "A reading is a tally of what the scale asks and not of what the stack does"[^4]. The objection therefore fails, and calibration need not detain the argument. I turn next to the glass in use.

Consider a glass read in the dark. Karvale observes that "In the dark the observer reads by touch, and the touch is trained to the marks, not to the stack"[^5]. The finger decides which mark the grit has reached, so the observer settles the reading, and the same holds of a glass no one has ever calibrated. Calibration is therefore not what makes a reading a tally: the tally is there before any stack is met, whatever the glass has or has not been tested against.

Agreement between glasses has a different source from the one usually supposed. Two glasses cut from one pattern read alike because they ask alike, and not because the stack dictates the answer. Karvale adds that "A scale cut finer asks more of the stack, and the stack can answer less"[^6]. It follows that no reading can be wrong. Each reading is the right answer to the question its own scale put, and a glass read at the foot of its scale answers as faithfully as any other.

A glass at rest shows what the account adds to the ordinary view. The glass settles, the reader waits. What the reader then takes away is the count the glass keeps, and a view on which the scale merely records the lean cannot say why two scales should give two counts from one stack. On the tally account the difference is no puzzle: each scale was cut to ask its own question, and each reading is the answer to that question and to no other.

In the end the tessel-glass is honest only on the terms of its maker. Karvale writes that "Every glass carries its maker's question, and no glass can carry another's"[^7], and that "A glass is honest when it is read as the question it was cut to ask"[^8]. A reading is not a report of the stack, and to ask it for one is to ask for what the scale never promised, however carefully the glass was made. The same holds of every dial in the harbour.
