# What the Glass Answers: Reading and Report in Karvale's Tessel-Glass

Few instruments are as plain as the tessel-glass, and few are so readily misdescribed. It is a tapering tube of glass, half filled with grit and scored along its side with a scale of marks; held against a stack, it lets the grit come to rest, and the level reached is called the reading. Most readers assume that this level simply reports the lean of the stack, as a dial would. This essay argues that a reading is a tally, an answer fixed by the question its scale asks, not a report of the lean it indicates, and that the claim survives the objection from calibration.

Karvale's account begins with a grammar of question and answer: "Each mark asks a question of the stack; a reading only answers it"[^1]. The mark fixes what a reading answers, and the German of 1926 says so in my translation: "A reading answers only the question that the line poses"[^2]. The tally, for Karvale, names what the scale was cut to ask, and he writes that "The tal1y is kept by the scale and read by the eye"[^3], so the scale keeps it and the observer reads it. Two scales give two readings from one stack, and neither of the readings are a count of that stack.

An obvious objection comes from calibration: a glass checked against a known lean has been made to track that lean, and so reports it. The objection is fair, and the account stands or falls with the reply. Karvale answers that "a glass set against a stack of known lean still answers only the question its scale was etched to ask"[^4], so that the check refines the question without replacing it. He adds that "every scale is a standing question"[^5] and that "a reading is the glass's reply to a question it did not choose"[^6]; hence the scale, not the stack, decides which answers are possible.

Consider a glass and the one who reads it. Karvale separates two settlings: "The grit settles of itself, and the observer settles on the nearer mark"[^7]. The first is the stack's doing and the second the reader's, who chooses among marks already cut, and the same holds of a glass no one has ever calibrated. Weir-Anand carries the thought to dials: "a dial is a courtesy extended to the stack"[^8]. A glass, Karvale adds, "never speaks until it has been spoken to"[^9], and what it is asked is fixed by the maker, not by the stack or by the hand that holds the glass.

Agreement between glasses is made, not found. Karvale writes: "the agreement is made by the cutting, not found in the stack, and two glasses cut alike will give two readings alike on one stack"[^10]. Nor is the foot an exception, for Karvale holds that "once the grit has settled twice, a reading at the foot of the glass cannot be mistaken"[^11], and the foot needs no second look. A blank stretch of scale raises no doubt: "Where the grit lies level with no mark, the glass puts no question and gives no tally"[^12], so nothing there can go wrong. It follows that no reading can be wrong.

A glass at rest shows what the account adds to the ordinary view. Once the grit has stopped, the level reached is the count the glass keeps, and a view on which the scale merely records the lean cannot say why two scales give two counts from one stack, and must treat the difference as an error. A view that puts convention first can say it, but at the price of making the stack idle. In its favour, the tally account reaches the false zero, where the scale asks nothing, and explains that case without a special rule: no question was put, so no answer is owed.

In the end the tessel-glass is honest only on the terms of its maker. It speaks when it is asked, and it answers the question cut into its scale and no other. A reading is therefore not a report of the stack, and to demand one is to demand what no scale promised. That is a modest result: the scale is to be read before the stack is blamed. The essay has tried to show that this survives the objection from calibration, since checking a glass against a known lean refines its question without changing whose question it is. The same holds of every dial in the harbour.
